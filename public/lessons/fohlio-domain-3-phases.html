How Buildings Get Furnished

Chapter 3. One chair’s journey: the real phases

The chair is already on a ship before Dana has finished the drawings.

That sentence sounds wrong. It is also true. The Austin hotel project is seven months into design development — walls are going up on site, the GC is running concrete — and Marco has already placed a purchase order for 240 lounge chairs. They are being fabricated in North Carolina right now. By the time the construction crew finishes the rooms, the chairs will be waiting in a warehouse twelve miles away, inspected and barcoded, ready to be carried in.

This is the central fact about FF&E that almost nobody outside the industry knows: procurement does not wait for construction to finish. It runs in parallel, on its own track, because the time it takes to order, make, ship, and inspect a chair is longer than the time it takes to build the room that will hold it. If you wait for the building to be done before you order the furniture, the hotel opens late. And a late opening, as we learned in Lesson 1, costs about $15,000 a day [1].

This chapter is the map of that parallel track, walked one chair at a time.

Part 1. The AIA vocabulary — why the phases have the names they do

Interior designers and architects use the same project-phase vocabulary, because for decades the American Institute of Architects has published contract documents — forms that define what the designer does in each phase and when. For FF&E specifically, the AIA publishes contracts B152, B153, and B252 — a dedicated interiors family that splits FF&E services into the same named phases as building design [2].

This matters for one practical reason: when a purchasing agent, a designer, and an owner’s rep are in the same room, they all use these phase names to mean the same thing. They are a shared lingua franca. If you know them, you can follow any project conversation. If you don’t, you spend half the meeting catching up.

The phases are: Programming → Schematic Design (SD) → Design Development (DD) → Construction Documents / FF&E Documents (CD) → Budgeting & Value Engineering → Bidding / RFQ → Purchase Orders (PO) → Expediting → Manufacturing → Freight & Import → Receiving & Warehousing → Delivery & Installation → Punch List → Closeout → OS&E & Operations.

Fifteen phases. One chair moves through all of them.

Part 2. The design half — phases 1 through 6

These are the phases where the chair does not exist yet. It is an idea, then a description, then a budget line, then a purchase order.

1 Programming (Pre-Design)
The studio establishes scope and preliminary budget before any product is chosen. For the Austin hotel: 180 rooms, what room mix (kings, queens, suites), how many F&B seats, and a rough FF&E budget per key. Is the property flagged? If so, which brand standard applies and which approved-vendor lists are mandatory? [3]
2 Schematic Design (SD)
Dana’s team produces furniture layouts using generic placeholder blocks — no specific products yet, just approximate size and type. They test ADA clearances and circulation. The budget carries a large contingency (25–30%). Dana attends HD Expo this month, walking every showroom with Priya and collecting tear sheets for the categories she knows she’ll need. [3]
3 Design Development (DD)
Specific product families get selected. Dana produces FF&E cut sheets with exact dimensions, finishes, and COM specifications — customer’s own material, meaning she has sourced the fabric separately and the manufacturer will upholster the chair in it. She coordinates against the brand standards. This is the phase where the specification is effectively built — and where a purchasing agent like Marco needs to be engaged, because value-engineering must happen now, before orders are placed. [3]
4 Construction Documents / FF&E Documents (CD)
The deliverable is the complete FF&E specification book: every item, by room type — model number, finish, fabric, dimensions, installation requirements, quantity. Alongside it, the FF&E schedule cross-references item code, quantity, unit cost, and total. At the end of this phase, RFQs go to shortlisted vendors. [3]

Schedule vs. Specification — the distinction that confuses everyone. The specification describes what a product is: model, finish, fabric, dimensions, manufacturer. The FF&E schedule tracks the project status of every product: vendor, quantity, unit cost, total, lead time, order status, delivery date, budget position. The specification is written once (and updated when something changes). The schedule has to be accurate right now, every day, for the life of the project. Dana’s $40k mistake happened because the specification changed — the fabric was discontinued — but the schedule was never updated to reflect it [4].

5 Budgeting & Value Engineering
Budgets are refined continuously through DD and CD. When the specified package comes in over budget — which it reliably does — the team runs value engineering: substituting equal-or-better, lower-cost alternates without sacrificing design intent. On flagged hotels, the brand may approve stock items from a major vendor to speed timelines and cut cost. This negotiation is Marco’s least glamorous and most important job. [3]
6 Bidding / RFQ
Marco sources 3–5 vendors per item category, comparing quality, price, and lead time. An RFQ (Request for Quotation) asks for price on an already-defined spec — the chair, in the exact finish and COM, at 240 units. Vendors return quotes; the team negotiates and selects. Tomás, the manufacturer’s rep, is usually the person on the other end of the call. [5]

Part 3. The parallel track — phases 7 through 15

Here is where the industry’s central logic becomes visible. Construction is still underway. The GC is building the shell. The floors are not poured. The rooms do not exist. And Marco cuts the purchase orders anyway.

This is not recklessness. It is the only way to open on time.

FF&E design and procurement are parallel tracks. POs are cut months before the building is done. For overseas or custom items, the order goes in before construction is even close to finished. Both tracks converge at installation, days before opening.

Lead times: the number that drives everything

If you remember one set of numbers from this lesson, let them be these. They are not estimates — they are the operational reality that every project schedule is built around [3]:

🇺🇸
6–10 weeks
Domestic furniture
Standard domestic contract furniture, already in stock or short-run production. The fastest category. Still six weeks minimum — not “I need it next week.”
🚢
18–24 weeks
Overseas production
China, Vietnam, Turkey — production plus ocean freight plus customs. Four to six months. A project that opens in Month 24 needs POs cut around Month 18 or earlier.
🔨
6–12 months
Custom / millwork
Bespoke headboards, custom millwork reception desks, handmade lighting. The longest-lead, highest-risk items. Order them at DD, not at CD. [3]
⏰
16–20 weeks total
Typical domestic end-to-end
Design & spec (2–4 wks) → approval (3–5 wks) → production (6–8 wks) → freight/customs (2–3 wks) → install (3–4 wks) ≈ 16–20 weeks total [3].
💬
Priya, the first time she hears the lead-time numbers: “Wait. Six months for a custom headboard? That’s before we even know what the room looks like.”
Marco: “No, that’s after you’ve designed it, specified it, had it approved by the owner and the brand, run value engineering, and gotten a confirmed quote. Then you wait six months.”
Priya: “What happens if it’s wrong?”
Marco: “You call Dana. Dana gets very quiet. We figure out if we can ship something that’s almost right.”

Part 4. Phases 7 through 15 — the procurement half

7 Purchase Orders (POs)
Marco converts approved selections into purchase orders — binding contracts with the vendor. Typically 50% deposit on the PO, 50% before or on delivery. Net 30 or Net 60 payment terms for established vendors; proforma (pay first) for new or overseas relationships. The PO is the moment the chair officially exists as a transaction. [4]
8 Expediting
After the PO is cut, nothing moves automatically. Marco, or someone on his team, actively chases each order: tracking factory progress, answering vendor questions, evaluating alternates when items are discontinued. This is the phase where Dana’s $40k mistake was born — the fabric went stale, the manufacturer substituted silently, and nobody was looking closely enough. [5]

This is where the $40k mistake arose. Expediting — Phase 8 — is the long silent gap after the PO and before the goods ship. It is the phase nobody outside the industry pictures when they think of “buying furniture.” The chair existed on paper (the spec), in a contract (the PO), and nominally in production — but nobody was watching the finish closely enough to catch when it changed. The mistake surfaced later, at Phase 11, when Reggie’s team inspected the arriving chairs at the warehouse. By then the only fix was a rework at $40,000.

9 Manufacturing & Lead Times
The factory produces the goods. For the Austin lounge chairs (domestic contract, COM fabric): 8 weeks from confirmed PO. For a custom millwork reception desk: 5 months. For imported lighting fixtures from a Turkish supplier: 22 weeks including ocean freight. Each item runs its own lead-time clock, independently, and they all have to converge at the warehouse in time for install. [3]
10 Freight & Import
Items ship via ocean, air, or road. Imported FF&E needs customs brokerage; specialist freight forwarders handle ocean/air/road plus in-house customs and warehousing. Logistics typically costs 6–25% of total FF&E product spend — an often-underestimated budget line. [5] In 2025–26, a tariff on a container of Chinese furniture can add tens of thousands of dollars to a project that was budgeted at pre-tariff rates.
11 Receiving & Warehousing
Goods from many manufacturers converge at a third-party warehouse. Reggie’s team inspects everything against the purchase orders, photographs each piece, and barcodes it. The output is a receiving report. Skipping this step — letting trucks go straight to site — is the single most common and most expensive logistics failure: damage discovered on install day instead of at the dock means reorders on a hard opening deadline. [5]
12 Delivery & Installation
Items are staged and delivered to site in install sequence. White-glove: unpack, place, assemble, remove debris. Scheduled 4–6 weeks before opening. Reggie’s crew works room by room, matching each piece to the hotel’s room-numbering scheme. The model room — a single fully-furnished room — goes first, so the owner and brand can sign off on the actual result before 179 more rooms are fitted. [5]
13 Punch List
A walkthrough generates the punch list — everything wrong or incomplete: damaged items, wrong finishes, missing pieces. The purchasing agent and GC resolve each item. Sam, the owner’s rep, typically co-signs the final inspection. A clean punch list is the last gate before opening. [4]
14 Closeout / Handover
Final accounting, reports, and a handover package: manuals, warranties, fire-rating certificates, and asset records transferred to the owner/operator. This is the paper trail that lives in a drawer for the next five to ten years, until the next renovation or PIP audit. Dana learned from the $40k mistake that the closeout record is also where “I never approved that” gets resolved — or doesn’t. [4]
15 OS&E & Operations
As we learned in Lesson 1, OS&E — the consumables layer (towels, linens, glassware, amenities) — runs on a parallel procurement track because it too is tied to the opening date. It starts later than FF&E (3–6 months before opening rather than 12–18), has vastly more SKUs, and never truly ends. It is the phase that outlasts everyone else on the project list. [1]

Part 5. What the mistake tells us about the pipeline

Let’s now place Dana’s $40k mistake precisely in the phase map.

The chair was specified correctly in Phase 3 (Design Development). The finish — a specific fabric SKU — was written into the spec. The purchase order was cut in Phase 7 with that finish on it. So far, everything was correct.

The mistake arose in Phase 8: Expediting. During the months between the PO and the shipment, the manufacturer quietly discontinued the exact fabric and substituted the “closest match.” Nobody caught it because the expediting process was not detailed enough to flag a finish substitution as requiring re-approval. The spec said one thing. The factory was making another. And the schedule was never updated.

The mistake surfaced in Phase 11: Receiving, when Reggie’s crew opened the containers at the warehouse and compared the delivered goods to the purchase orders. The finish was wrong. By then the options were: ship the chairs to site anyway and hope the owner doesn’t notice (they did), or pay for reupholstery on sixty chairs. The bill came to forty thousand dollars.

The spec was right the day Dana sent it. It stopped being right somewhere in Phase 8, and nobody noticed until Phase 11. That is the gap the whole industry is built around closing.

Part 6. The three client types in the phase timeline

One more layer of complexity, and then we’re done for today. The fifteen-phase pipeline does not run the same way for every project. Dana’s three client types — Linda (luxury flag), Brother Andersen (institutional), and Grace (independent boutique) — experience radically different versions of the same pipeline.

Phase cluster Linda (luxury flag) Brother Andersen (institutional) Grace (independent boutique)
Programming – DD Brand standards from the flag constrain product choices from Phase 1. Approved vendor lists. Linda must sign off at every gate. Long committee process. Scope changes slowly. Budget is strict from donated funds. Programming alone can take months. Grace decides the vibe over a phone call and a Pinterest board. No formal gates. Fast.
Bidding – PO Purchasing agent (Marco-type) runs a formal RFQ. POs issued after brand approval. Deposit terms strictly documented. Sometimes a formal procurement process. Often slow to approve POs because of internal sign-off chains. Grace calls one or two vendors she knows. Pays by credit card where possible. PO is a word document, if it exists at all.
Expediting – Receiving Professional logistics firm (Reggie-type). Receiving report, photos, barcodes. Model room sign-off by brand before full install. Expediting often handled by Dana on behalf of an under-resourced client. Receiving may be informal if no purchasing agent is engaged. Grace is usually the receiving crew. She’s also the expediting crew. If something goes wrong in Phase 8, she finds out in Phase 11, alone, on her phone.
Closeout Full warranty package, FR certificates, asset register. Brand may audit at any point afterward. Minimal if no purchasing agent. Dana often assembles the closeout documents herself. Whatever Grace can find in her email inbox. No asset register. No backstop for the next PIP — because there is no PIP.

Grace’s project is both the simplest and the most fragile. She skips most of the process friction that Linda and Brother Andersen have to navigate — but she also skips most of the checkpoints that catch mistakes. For a small inn, a $40k rework bill is not an inconvenience; it is a crisis.
