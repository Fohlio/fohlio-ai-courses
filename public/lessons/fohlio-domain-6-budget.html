Lesson 6 of 10

Money: spec ≠ budget ≠ actual

Chapter 6. Three different numbers about the same chairs

Dana lays two sheets of paper on the table. One says $187,000 — that’s the number Linda from Marriott signed off on last quarter. The other says $214,000. That’s what Marco ordered three weeks ago. Twenty-seven thousand dollars apart, and not a single line item in the spec has changed.

Priya stares at both numbers. Then: “Is one of them wrong?”

Neither is wrong. They’re answering three completely different questions about the same chairs. “What do they cost in the spec” is one number. “What did we order them for” is another. “What have we actually paid” is a third. In a spreadsheet, all three live on separate tabs and never talk to each other. In Fohlio, they live in the same place and are required to explain the gap. That gap is called variance — and it’s exactly what costs projects money when nobody is watching it.

What you’ll understand by the end of this lesson

  • Why spec total, planned, and actual are always three different numbers — and what each one actually answers
  • What markup and soft costs are, and how they open the gap between a supplier’s catalog price and the invoice total
  • How value engineering works: when “cheaper” doesn’t mean “worse”
  • What byArea and bySchedule mean as two different lenses on the same budget
  • Where Budget, variance, and the rule “actual = invoices, not orders” live in the domain model

Part 1. Three numbers, three questions

Marco thinks in numbers by default. So when he needs to explain the gap to Priya, he picks one example and doesn’t let go of it until she can reconstruct the math herself.

“Say a chair costs $400 in the supplier’s catalog. Dana specs 150 of them for Marriott. What’s spec total?”

Priya: “150 × 400 = $60,000?”

“Exactly. That’s the number Linda approved: the sum of all line items at catalog prices at the moment of sign-off. It’s an intent — a declaration. Now I go to the supplier, negotiate a 5% discount, but I also add freight and installation. What’s planned?”

“The sum of the purchase orders?”

“The sum of all PurchaseOrder records. Sometimes it’s above spec total, sometimes below. And after the trucks arrive and I receive the invoices, actual shows up. Three numbers — three different truths about the same chairs.”

In the model: Budget lives at the Project level. Fields: specTotal (sum from the spec slots), planned (sum of confirmed POs), actual (sum of paid invoices), variance = planned − specTotal. Source: src-baghdad-schema [4].
🤔 Estimate before you see the diagram
Spec total for those 150 chairs is $60,000. The supplier gives a 5% discount, but Marco adds $3,500 freight and $2,000 installation. Will planned be higher or lower than $60,000? By how much? Answer out loud or in writing, then check against the waterfall below.
Hint: the discount saves $3,000, but the soft costs add $5,500.
Variance = planned − specTotal. Positive means over the approved budget; negative means saved. Actual only appears after invoices are paid.

The key takeaway: spec total is not the budget. It’s a declaration of intent. Budget is planned — the real financial commitment made through purchase orders. Actual is the fact on the ground: money out of the account. A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper [1] — and the budget is just one of the output reports from that same database, not a separate file that lives in someone’s inbox.

Part 2. Where the gap comes from: markup and soft costs

Priya looks at $62,500 and frames the question more precisely: “The supplier quoted $57,000. Where does the extra five thousand come from?” Good question — and exactly the one that burns projects when nobody asks it early enough.

The gap has two sources.

Markup — the firm’s margin

The design studio buys a chair from a supplier for $400. The client gets billed $480 — a 20% markup. This isn’t hidden: the firm carries risk, manages the process, and guarantees compliance with the spec. For Marriott, markup may be negotiated in the franchise agreement; for Grace it may be absent entirely (she buys direct). In Fohlio, markup is a field on the slot or item that feeds into the planned calculation [4].

Soft costs — everything that isn’t the product

🚚
Freight
Supplier to site or warehouse. Grows non-linearly with weight and distance — heavy custom pieces can cost more to ship than to make.
🔨
Installation
Often a percentage of product cost, or a fixed rate per item. A 200-room hotel has a lot of items.
📦
Packing & handling
Packaging, unloading, moving through the site. Especially painful on custom pieces that arrive without standard pallets.
💸
Import duties & taxes
Customs duties, sales tax. On imported furniture: 5–25% on top. Depends entirely on country of origin and HS code [2].
💰
Client, looking at the invoice: “The chair was $400. I’m being billed $631. What kind of markup is this?”
Marco: “Freight $65, installation $48, duty $78, packing $40.”
Client: “Fine, that’s $631?”
Marco: “Plus markup 20%.”
Soft costs aren’t hidden. They’re the costs people forget to include in the approval budget, then “discover” on the invoice.

This is exactly why specTotal and planned are separate fields in Fohlio, not one. Spec total is catalog price × quantity. Planned already includes markup and the soft costs Marco added when building the PO. If they were the same field, you couldn’t compute variance until the invoices arrived — which is far too late to do anything about it.

✋ Explain without looking back
Name three examples of soft costs and explain why you can’t just “add 10% on top” — what specifically makes that estimate unreliable? Answer in two sentences.
Hint: think back to Lesson 3 — a Source on a catalog item includes the supplier’s location. Freight depends on where an item comes from and where it’s going.

Part 3. Value engineering — when the budget says no

Brother Andersen opens the budget for a 200-seat meetinghouse and sees that spec total is $18,000 over the approved number. He doesn’t raise the cap. He calls Dana.

A fixed budget is doctrine for The Church of Jesus Christ — not because of stinginess, but because it’s stewardship of donated funds. Every dollar over the estimate requires an explanation to the building committee. So when spec total doesn’t fit, the only path is value engineering: find line items that can be swapped for a less expensive equivalent without losing function or violating the standard.

Dana opens the chair slot and sees Herman Miller at $620 per unit — 120 units. In the Church’s approved standard, there’s an equivalent from HON at $310: same durability class, same fabric, same fire-safety rating. The swap holds the standard and saves $37,200 — more than enough to close the gap.

In the model: the swap happens through the alternatives list on ProjectItemSlot. Each slot has a primary item and a list of alternatives. Dana selects an alternative as primary; specTotal recomputes immediately. The standard isn’t violated as long as the alternative is in the Brand Standard [4].

For Marriott, value engineering works differently. Linda can’t simply substitute something cheaper, because the brand book pins specific models. But Marco can play on volume: Marriott is ordering the same models across many properties in this rollout simultaneously, which gives real leverage with the manufacturer. The result is the same — planned below spec total — through a different mechanism. Variance goes negative: they came in under budget.

Grace doesn’t have a brand standard to protect and she doesn’t have Marriott’s volume. Her value engineering is: “I found something similar on another site for $280.” The difference: Grace leaves no record of why she swapped. If she opens a second inn next year, she starts from scratch. The alternatives field with flags and comments in Fohlio exists precisely so that decision history doesn’t live only in someone’s memory.

🤔 Predict the downstream effect
Dana found an alternative chair — $90 cheaper per unit, 80 units. But it comes from a different supplier (recall Lesson 3: a price source is a Source record on the catalog item). How does the supplier change affect Marco’s next step — procurement? What does he now have to do that he didn’t have to do before?
Each item in the spec has a Source. If the source changes, what does Marco need from the new supplier before he can build a PO?

Part 4. Currencies, duties, taxes — why the “same” price isn’t

Priya opens the catalog and finds Italian tile. The manufacturer lists €45/m². Dana specs 800 m². Priya multiplies: €36,000. She calls Marco with that number. Marco is quiet for a second. Then, slowly: “Your PO is going to show a different number.”

Italian imports into the US carry several layers that don’t appear on the manufacturer’s catalog page [2]:

Layer Example Who adds it
EUR → USD exchange rate ×1.09 on the PO date The market — unpredictable at spec time
Import duty (customs) +4.5% for ceramic tile from the EU US Customs, based on HS code
Freight (FOB destination) +8–12% of product value Logistics provider, depends on weight and route
Sales tax (by state) 0–9.5% depending on state and category Tax law, varies widely
Studio markup 15% of landed cost Client contract

That €36,000 tile in the spec can become $55,000+ in planned — a 35–40% gap. Not an error. The reality of international procurement. This is why experienced specifiers on imported items enter landed cost — the full cost to site — rather than the manufacturer’s price, at least as a planning estimate.

FOB and Incoterms: FOB (Free on Board) means the supplier is responsible for the goods until they’re loaded onto the vessel. After that, risk and cost transfer to the buyer. FOB Origin vs FOB Destination is a critical distinction: FOB Origin means the buyer pays freight; FOB Destination means the supplier does, and the price already includes it. In Fohlio, the Source record on a catalog item holds the supplier’s terms, including shipping conditions [4].

Part 5. byArea and bySchedule — two lenses on one budget

Marriott wants to know: what does it cost to furnish one standard room? That’s a byArea question. Brother Andersen wants to know: how much is the Church spending on the “Seating” category across all meetinghouses? That’s a bySchedule question. Same budget, two completely different cuts through it.

The brand standards from Lesson 5 [5] are doing real work here. If Linda approved specific items for the standard room, then Budget byArea immediately shows the cost per room and what 600 rooms will total. Any deviation from the standard — a brand-book violation — shows up immediately as a positive variance at the Area level [4].

🏨
byArea
Slice by room / zone
“What does the Lobby cost?” “What does one guest room cost?” Useful for Marriott (repeating units) and Grace (12 rooms she can price individually).
📋
bySchedule
Slice by category
“What are we spending on Furniture?” “On Lighting?” Useful for category-level budget caps and procurement tenders where one vendor covers a whole category.

For Grace, both slices collapse into the same answer: she has 12 rooms and three categories. She looks at total variance and reacts. There’s no time for two reports. This is why Fohlio surfaces the budget in both cuts: different clients ask different questions of the same underlying database, and the database has to answer all of them.

Part 6. Three clients, one variance problem

Friday at 4:40 p.m. Dana has three projects open on her screen. Each one has its own story about money.

1 The Church — fixed budget and value engineering
Brother Andersen calls every two weeks: “How’s the budget?” Not “how’s the design” — specifically “how’s the budget.” The Church has an approved list (the Brand Standard from Lesson 5). If spec total goes above the limit, value engineering is not optional — it’s the only response. Dana cannot offer “nicer, but more expensive.” Only “same quality and standard, lower cost.” Variance here is a warning that must be resolved before a single PO is created.
2 Marriott — markup and the franchise corridor
Linda manages the budget through markup. Marriott’s central procurement charges the design firm for process management. The contract specifies a corridor: markup no higher than 18%. Above that, Linda renegotiates. Below 8%, the studio is working at a loss. Marco’s job is to land inside the corridor on every category. Variance at Marriott shows up as a quarterly review line on each property.
3 Grace — counting every dollar
Grace opened a credit line for $85,000. That’s her hard cap. When planned creeps toward $80,000, she calls Dana on Sunday. She has no dedicated buyer, no Marco, no CFO. She has the Fohlio budget view and a real fear of crossing the line. For Grace, variance isn’t a quarterly metric — it’s a live anxiety readout. The budget dashboard exists for her not as a report, but as a stress-management panel.

Chapter summary

Three numbers, three questions about the same line items — this isn’t bureaucracy. It’s the only way to keep a thousand-row spec under control from intent to invoice.

📊
Three numbers
specTotal (intent) → planned (committed) → actual (paid). Variance = planned − specTotal.
➕
Markup + soft costs
Freight, installation, duties, taxes — turn the catalog price into a real planned figure. Forget them and your estimate is off by 30–40%.
🔧
Value engineering
Replace an expensive item with a cheaper equivalent — without violating the standard. The mechanism: alternatives on the slot.
📌
byArea / bySchedule
Two slices of the same budget. Different clients ask different questions of the same database.
A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper. The budget is a query against it. And variance is what happens when the query returns something different from what you expected.

    • Armchair: $480 × 24 units
    • Light fixture: $220 × 36 units
    • Rug: $340 × 12 units

    (a) Calculate spec total. (b) Marco adds freight $2,800, installation $3,200, and a 15% markup on the goods. Calculate planned and variance. (c) Grace pays for the beds and armchairs in the first wire transfer — what is actual after that payment? (d) Which budget slice — byArea or bySchedule — matters more to Grace, and why?

    AI-free recall: before you start calculating, close this lesson and spend 60 seconds dictating the definitions of specTotal, planned, actual, and variance. Then open back up and check yourself.

    ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    (a) specTotal = (1,200×12) + (480×24) + (220×36) + (340×12) = 14,400 + 11,520 + 7,920 + 4,080 = $37,920. (b) Markup 15%: 37,920 × 1.15 = $43,608; + freight $2,800 + installation $3,200 = planned = $49,608. Variance = 49,608 − 37,920 = +$11,688. (c) Actual covers beds + armchairs. Their portion of planned (proportional to spec value + markup + attributed soft costs) is roughly (14,400 + 11,520) × 1.15 + proportional soft costs. Passes if actual < planned with correct reasoning — not all items have been paid. (d) Grace cares more about byArea (“what does one room cost?”) — she thinks in 12 individual rooms, not procurement categories. She has no category tender to run.
  2. Cross-lesson question (roughly half from prior lessons). Dana found an alternative armchair for Cedar & Oak: a different supplier (a Source from Lesson 3), no Brand Standard constraint (Grace doesn’t have one), but the new supplier only sells in bulk — minimum order quantity (MOQ) is 30 units, and Dana only needs 24. Answer three questions:
    • What happens to variance when the armchair price drops from $480 to $360 per unit?
    • How does switching suppliers (Lesson 3) break Marco’s work if the new supplier has no quote on file?
    • Can value engineering create a new budget problem if MOQ exceeds the required quantity?
    ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    The swap lowers specTotal by (480−360)×24 = $2,880, reducing variance all else equal. But if Marco has no quote from the new supplier, he can’t compute planned for that item — there’s no Source price to build a PO against. A new Source means a new RFQ (covered in Lesson 7). MOQ = 30 when qty = 24 means Marco orders 30 units; planned grows by 6 × unit cost + markup. Value engineering saved on the unit price, but MOQ partially takes back the saving through extra units — unless Dana finds a supplier without a MOQ constraint.
