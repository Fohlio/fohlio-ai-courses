RFQs, Submittals and Samples

Chapter 10. The paperwork that proves the chair is what you think it is

Dana spreads three fabric swatches on the conference table. All three came back from vendors claiming they match the spec. All three look roughly the same in the room. Under the window, in actual daylight, they are visibly different shades of walnut. One is brown. One is warm grey. One has a red cast that will fight the carpet the moment anyone turns a lamp on.

The vendor behind the brown one is convinced they sent the right thing. Dana is convinced they did not. The spec says “Walnut, medium tone, matte finish.” It does not say which of these three is that. It never occurred to anyone to ask. And because nobody asked before the order went in — because there was no moment in the process where someone held an actual sample against an actual light source and said “this, not that” — the project is now two weeks from a purchase order and three months from an install, and nobody knows which chair they’re buying.

This is what submittals exist to prevent. And this is why the industry invented the sample approval process long before anyone computerized it. Dana learned the hard way in Lesson 1 what happens when a spec stops being true. Today, we’re in the weeks just before that purchase order — the window where the spec gets tested against reality one last time before the money moves.

Part 1. Getting a price — RFQ vs. RFP

Before Dana can issue a purchase order, someone has to know what things cost. That sounds obvious. It is less obvious which tool to use to find out.

The two standard documents for soliciting vendor responses are an RFQ and an RFP, and the difference is not just vocabulary. It tells a vendor how much homework they’re expected to do.

An RFQ — Request for Quotation — says: “We know exactly what we want. Tell us your price.” The spec is already written. The vendor fills in a number. This is the document Dana’s firm sends when the specification is finalized and the team just needs competitive pricing across three to five shortlisted vendors [1].

An RFP — Request for Proposal — says: “We have a need. Propose how you’d solve it.” It invites the vendor to explain their approach, their product offering, their lead times, their quality standards, their references — not just their price. RFPs appear when the specification is still open or when the owner wants to evaluate vendors on criteria beyond cost alone. A hotel buying a procurement agent’s services (as we saw with the Osage Casino RFQ in Lesson 8) will often run an RFP precisely because price alone doesn’t capture whether the firm can handle the complexity [1].

The FF&E workflow reality: for a hotel project, most line items go out as RFQs. The spec is already written (Dana spent three months on it), and what she needs is prices to validate her budget estimates and choose among vendors. But if she’s replacing a product category where the spec is deliberately loose — “find us a contemporary lounge chair for the lobby” — she might run an RFP to gather proposals with samples, allowing the selection to happen after she sees what comes back.

In practice, the purchasing agent runs both processes simultaneously. Tomás — the manufacturer’s rep Dana has worked with since the vendor deep-dive in Lesson 9 — knows to expect a set of RFQs around week twelve of a design phase. He keeps his quote templates ready. What he cannot do is tell Dana the price without knowing the finish, the quantity, the fabric specification, and whether the project has any brand-standard constraints. An RFQ without a tight spec is just a conversation with extra paperwork.

Think about what’s already decided in each case — and what the vendor is being asked to contribute.

Once quotes come back — usually within one to two weeks for standard items, longer for custom — the team evaluates on price, lead time, and vendor-qualification criteria. The shortest quote time does not always win. A vendor offering a two-week lead time that turned out to be aspirational cost Marco three weeks of expediting chaos on the last project. He now weights stated lead times against each vendor’s track record from prior work [2].

Part 2. What a submittal actually is

The word “submittal” has a specific meaning in the construction world, and it is not the same as “sending something to someone.” Under AIA A201 — the standard contract that governs the relationship between architects, contractors, and owners on virtually every formal construction project in the US — a submittal is a document or sample that a contractor or vendor submits to the design team to prove conformance before fabrication or installation happens [3].

The logic is this: the designer drew something; the contractor is about to build or install it; before they do, they must show the designer that what they propose to use is actually what was specified. A submittal is not the vendor saying “trust us.” It is the vendor proving it, with documentation.

What submittals look like

They come in three forms, and each answers a slightly different question:

📋
Product data
Cut sheets, spec sheets, material data. “Here is what this product is.”
📐
Shop drawings
Detailed fabrication drawings the contractor prepares. “Here is exactly how we will build it.”
🧵
Physical samples
Fabric swatches, material boards, paint chips, finish panels. “Here is what it will look like in real life.”

Shop drawings in particular are worth pausing on. These are not the designer’s drawings. They are drawings prepared by the contractor or the vendor, showing in detail how they plan to fabricate and install something. By submitting them, the contractor is legally certifying — per AIA A201 §3.12.6 — that they have checked the field dimensions, verified the conditions, and confirmed the work will fit. The designer’s review does not relieve the contractor of that responsibility. It only confirms that the approach is consistent with the design intent [3].

For FF&E, shop drawings appear most often on custom millwork — the reception desk, the headboards, the built-in bar cabinetry. These are the items with no off-the-shelf catalog equivalent, where the fabricator is working from the designer’s concept drawings and translating them into actual construction dimensions. Reviewing those drawings carefully, before the wood is cut, is how Dana avoids a headboard that’s twelve inches too wide for a room with a fire sprinkler in the wrong position.

Part 3. The four review outcomes — and why the language is precise

When a designer or architect reviews a submittal, they return one of four standard responses. The language is consistent across the industry because it has contractual weight. “I liked it” is not a response. Neither is “looks good.” The four actions are [3]:

The four submittal review actions, per AIA A201. “Approved as Noted” is the most common outcome on a complex FF&E item.

A few things worth knowing about this review: it is “limited.” Specifically, the architect reviews only “for conformance with the design concept” — the designer is not certifying the contractor’s means, methods, or field dimensions. If the contractor submits a shop drawing that will cause a structural problem the designer cannot see from that drawing, that is still the contractor’s liability. What the designer is saying is: this appears to be what I specified [3].

Priya, reviewing a submittal: “It says ‘Approved as Noted’ on the stamp. What note?”
Dana: “Flip it over.”
Priya flips it over. There are seventeen handwritten notes in margins so small she needs to zoom in on the scan.
Dana: “Welcome to submittal review. ‘Approved’ means proceed. ‘Approved as Noted’ means proceed, but read every single note, do every single thing in those notes, and if one is unclear, send an RFI before you do anything else.”
The industry term for ignoring the notes is “change order.”

Part 4. The CFA sample — the dye-lot guarantee

Dana has three fabric swatches on the table. She’s been here before. Lesson 1’s $40k mistake was a fabric that got discontinued and silently substituted after the spec was set. The industry’s solution to that specific problem — the one that creates defensible documentation that “we agreed on this color, from this run” — is the CFA sample.

CFA stands for “Cutting-for-Approval” — a piece of fabric cut from the actual dye lot, the actual production run, that will be used on the finished goods. Not a sample from the showroom floor. Not a swatch card that represents a colorway in general. The specific fabric, from the specific bolt or batch, that the manufacturer will put on the chairs [4].

This distinction matters because fabrics are dye-lot sensitive. Two bolts of the same SKU in the same colorway from the same manufacturer, made six months apart, can read as different colors in person. Furniture upholstered from different dye lots in the same project will not match. And if the CFA was approved but the delivered goods used a different dye lot, there is a record. The designer can point to it and say: “I approved this. What arrived is not this.”

Without a CFA, both sides are arguing from memory. With one, there is a physical artifact that settles the dispute — assuming anyone kept it. Which is why Helen, the studio principal, has a drawer full of labeled and dated fabric samples for every project her firm has completed in the last eight years. She calls it “the drawer that saves us from lawyers.”

The CFA process: vendor sends the CFA swatch, the designer holds it next to the spec reference and the project color palette, signs and dates it, and returns a copy to the vendor. The vendor ships it to the factory with the order. The factory upholsters against the approved CFA. If the finished goods deviate, the factory is responsible — not the designer, not the owner. The CFA is the moment the accountability shifts.

CFA samples also apply to materials beyond fabric: paint chips from the specific production batch, veneer panels from the actual wood stock, powder-coat panels from the metal finish line. Anywhere dye-lot or batch variation is a real risk, a physical approval sample is the defense. Brand standards teams at flagged hotels, remembering Linda’s rigidity from Lesson 6, are often even more demanding about this than the designers are. Consistency across forty rooms is only possible if everyone is working from the same approved reference.

Part 5. RFIs — when the spec is unclear

An RFI is a Request for Information. It is not a submittal, and understanding why the two tools exist for different reasons matters more than memorizing their names.

A submittal says: “Here is what we propose to use. Please confirm it conforms to the spec.”

An RFI says: “The spec is unclear (or missing, or conflicting). Please tell us what you actually want.”

When a contractor gets a spec that says “provide a chair in a warm medium wood finish” without a specific product or finish code, they cannot write a submittal because they do not know what to propose. They issue an RFI first: which finish? The designer answers, and now the contractor can produce a submittal against the confirmed specification [3].

The two processes often run concurrently and interact. A submittal can spawn an RFI if reviewing the shop drawing reveals an ambiguity in the documents. An RFI answer can require a new or revised submittal. In a well-run project, both get logged in the same tracking system so nothing falls through a gap between the two [5].

Tool Direction The question it answers When it’s used
RFQ Owner/designer → vendor “What’s your price for this?” Spec is written; need competitive pricing
RFP Owner/designer → vendor “How would you approach this?” Spec is open; evaluating approach, not just price
Submittal Vendor/contractor → designer “Does this conform to the spec?” Before fabrication; proving the product or drawing is correct
RFI Vendor/contractor → designer “What do you actually want?” Spec is ambiguous, missing, or conflicting
CFA sample Vendor → designer → vendor “Is this the exact material, from this exact run?” Before mass fabrication; dye-lot or batch-dependent goods

Part 6. The two approval loops

Here is where most people who are new to the industry get confused, because two completely separate approval conversations are happening at the same time, and they involve different people, move through different documents, and have different stakes if they stall.

The first is Loop A: designer and owner. This is the conversation where the designer presents selected products to the client — the owner, the hotel brand representative, the operator — for sign-off on the design intent. Did we pick the right chair? Is the lobby color palette approved? Does this match the brand? Each design phase has a gate: sign-off on the concept before moving to development, sign-off on development before issuing specifications. On flagged hotels, the brand (Linda’s world) also sits in this loop, comparing every selection against the brand standard from Lesson 6 [2].

The second is Loop B: designer or contractor and vendor. This is the submittal conversation. Has the vendor proven that what they’re proposing or producing matches what was specified? This loop is about technical conformance, not aesthetic choice. A chair can be aesthetically approved by the owner in Loop A and still fail Loop B if the vendor’s shop drawing shows the wrong dimensions.

Loop A is about “did we choose the right thing?” Loop B is about “are we getting the thing we chose?” A product can pass one and fail the other.

The practical consequence of conflating these loops is a project in which the owner approved the design package but nobody confirmed that the vendor’s product data and shop drawings matched the spec. Or conversely, where the contractor submitted and the designer approved the submission, but nobody showed the owner what was actually being ordered until it arrived. Neither mistake is hypothetical. Both are named failure modes in how the industry works.

We will do a full treatment of both loops in Lesson 12 — including the model room sign-off, the punch list, and why the audit trail matters when someone says “I never approved that.” For now, hold the distinction: design loop = was this the right choice?; submittal loop = is this the thing we chose?

A brief Fohlio aside: platforms that manage FF&E specifications try to serve both loops in one workspace — designers share tear sheets and schedules with owners for design approval, and vendors are invited into a scoped view to provide their cut sheets, product data, and sample confirmations back. The value is keeping both conversations in one traceable record rather than scattered across email threads, where it becomes impossible to answer “who approved what, and when?” This is the direction the industry is moving, though in 2026 a majority of firms still manage it by email and shared drives.
