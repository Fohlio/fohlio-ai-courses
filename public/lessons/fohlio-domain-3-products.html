Chapter 3. Web clipper, families, and why the catalog is the firm’s library

Priya opens the Herman Miller website and lands on the Aeron chair page. Four frame colors, three sizes, two armrest types, two back heights. She does the multiplication in her head. One chair on the page — twenty-four distinct configurations.

“How do I add this to the spec?” she asks.

Dana looks at her for a moment. “Depends on what exactly you’re adding.” She points at the screen. “That” — gesturing at the product page — “is a family. This chair — graphite, size B, 4D arms — is one item in that family. And the item is what goes in the spec. Not the picture, not the page title. The specific configuration.”

Priya nods. Then looks at the screen again: twenty-four configurations. “Do we enter all of them by hand?”

That question is the opening of a longer conversation about the catalog — about how product data gets into the system in the first place, how it’s structured, and how it gets reused across the next project, and the one after that. Last lesson we followed the path from design intent to the first product candidate. This lesson covers what happens next: how the data is saved, organized, and made available to the whole firm.

Part 1. The data-entry problem: 14 email formats

Before we talk about the catalog structure, there’s a more basic question: where does product data come from? The obvious answer is the supplier. The practical answer is messier.

A supplier might send: a PDF catalog, a Word document, an Excel price list, a product page URL, an account manager’s email, a tearsheet, a BIM object for Revit, a Google Sheet with SKUs. Every supplier has their own format. Every format uses different field names. One writes the article number as SKU, another as Model#, a third buries it in column four with no header.

📬
Priya: “Why do we need a web clipper? I’ll just copy the data from the supplier’s email.”
Dana: “Sure. Here are 14 emails from 14 suppliers on the Marriott project. Each one has a different template. Three don’t include the price — you’ll need to request it separately. Two have no SKU, only a product name. One has a SKU but it’s written with spaces instead of hyphens, so it doesn’t match anything on the supplier’s website.”
Copying from an email is work you redo with every new request. A web clipper structures the data once — and then the whole firm reuses it in every project that follows.

The web clipper solves exactly this: it extracts structured data from a supplier’s product page and maps it into the fields Fohlio actually needs — name, SKU, finishes, images, pricing — tied to the correct product. Not a copy-paste of an email. An actual data record [3].

Part 2. CatalogFamily and CatalogItem: not one chair, but twenty-four

Back to Priya’s Aeron. Why can’t we just save “the Aeron chair” as a single object?

Because “the Aeron chair” is not a specific thing. It’s a family. Across different projects, it appears in different configurations: graphite, size B in one; white, size A in another. If every configuration were stored as its own product, the catalog would turn into noise within a year — two hundred entries all named some variant of “Aeron Chair,” with no relationship between them, duplicated data, and prices that drift out of sync.

That’s why Fohlio separates the model from the variant: CatalogFamily is the parent model, CatalogItem is a specific configuration (SKU) [4].

Family — the parent model. Items — specific SKU variants. Sources — suppliers with pricing. Parts — sub-components (BOM).
🤔 Predict before reading on
Suppose Herman Miller changes the price for the entire Aeron line. In this structure, where does that update need to happen to keep all 24 variants current — at the FamilySource level or separately on each ItemSource? Think about which one an account manager would actually call to give you a revised price sheet.
Hint: trace what data lives at the FamilySource level versus the ItemSource level, and think about what that means for Marco’s workload when a supplier updates their entire catalog.

The answer depends on where the price was attached in the first place. If the price sits on FamilySource, one update covers the whole family. If each variant has its own ItemSource with an individual price, each one needs a separate update. This architectural choice directly determines how much work Marco does every time Herman Miller sends a revised price sheet.

Part 3. The catalog is the firm’s library, not a data warehouse

Now — why all of this structure lives at the Workspace level rather than inside each project.

Dana doesn’t work on one project. She has three right now, and a year from now she’ll have seven. In most of them, some Herman Miller product will appear. If the catalog existed only per project, she’d re-enter the same chair data every time — run the clipper again, check the SKU again, re-confirm the supplier. Work already done, done again.

Instead, the catalog is shared across the entire Workspace — meaning the whole firm. Find a product once, add it to the library, and the next project simply pulls from it [4].

A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper.

The same logic applies to the catalog: it’s not an archive of product documents. It’s a live database where every product is a structured record with a supplier, finishes, pricing, and a history of use across projects. Helen — who founded the studio and controls firm standards — treats catalog quality as a firm reputation issue. A poorly maintained catalog means every specifier does extra manual verification instead of trusting data that’s already been vetted [1].

Catalog status is not a UI decoration

Every product in the catalog carries a status: draft — just created, not yet reviewed; new_from_project — pulled in from a project, awaiting team review; ready_to_use — approved, safe to use in new projects.

Helen reviews the catalog and promotes products to ready_to_use only after verification. Dana can’t put anything she wants into production — status first, trust second.

Engineering angle: CatalogStatus is a data quality gate, not a display flag. A product in draft can be referenced in a project but won’t surface as an “approved” option during search. This matters critically for segments with brand standards (Marriott, the Church): only ready_to_use products pass the standard-compliance check.

Part 4. Parts and BOM: when a chair isn’t just a chair

Sometimes a product is made of sub-components that are ordered, tracked, or swapped out separately. Legs in a different finish than the seat. A tabletop from one source, a base from another. COM fabric — Customer’s Own Material — where the client provides the textile themselves.

In Fohlio this is called Parts, and it’s the domain equivalent of a BOM (Bill of Materials) from manufacturing. Each Part is itself a CatalogFamily — a full product with its own variants and suppliers. A custom bed might consist of four parts, each arriving from a different vendor [4].

One important clarification: Parts are not mandatory. The majority of line items in a real project are monolithic — no sub-components. Parts exist for situations where the procurement or installation process genuinely requires tracking pieces independently. Grace’s 12-room inn almost certainly doesn’t need them. Marriott’s custom guestroom beds — where headboard and base ship from different suppliers — almost certainly do.

Part 5. Three clients, one concept

How three clients populate the catalog — and what each pattern says about their underlying model of work.

Marriott: pulls from an approved list

Marriott has a pre-approved product list. Dana doesn’t search Herman Miller’s website — she opens Linda’s brand list and selects from what’s already vetted. No web clipper needed: products are already in the system with ready_to_use status. Her job is to pick the right variant (CatalogItem) for the specific project. For Marriott, the catalog is the brand book in machine-readable form.

This segment has the highest data quality bar: no draft products in Linda’s production list. Any deviation from the approved list requires formal sign-off. A spec that references an unapproved product doesn’t get built — it gets sent back.

Grace: clips ad-hoc

Grace found a chair on a supplier site she’d never worked with. Dana runs the web clipper, pulls the data from the page, and saves it as a new Family in the catalog with new_from_project status. Helen will see it, review it, and either promote it to ready_to_use or flag it — “this supplier is unreliable, find an alternative.” For Cedar & Oak, the catalog grows organically, one project at a time.

SMB clients build catalogs bottom-up, from projects outward — not top-down from an approved master list. That’s a different search pattern and a different UI flow. A screen designed only for Marriott’s “select from approved list” mode would leave Grace with nowhere to put the chair she just found.

The Church: reuses a fixed kit

Brother Andersen wants the same meetinghouse they built last time. The products from that project are already in the system. Dana opens the workspace catalog, filters for the “standard meetinghouse” tag, and sees the full set of previously used products. Her task: verify that each one is still current — ready_to_use status, supplier unchanged, finish not discontinued. That exact verification step is where the $40k risk lives. The catalog either tells her the finish is still available or it doesn’t. Lesson 8 is about building the mechanism that answers that question reliably.

For institutional clients, catalog value isn’t discovery — it’s reliable reuse of known-good products. That’s a sync problem, not a search problem. The two are very different to build.

The three clients use the catalog in fundamentally different ways. And what breaks for Linda if the interface is designed only for Grace — open search across the whole web? Answer both directions before reading on.

Part 6. From web clipper to spec line: the full path

Pull everything together. Dana found the right chair for Grace. What happens technically from “found it on the supplier site” to “line in the spec”?

# Product path: supplier website → ProjectItemSlot 1. Web clipper └─ parses the supplier product page └─ extracts: name, SKU, finishes, images, price └─ creates CatalogFamily (status: new_from_project) 2. Family setup └─ Dana reviews / adds CatalogItems (variants) └─ attaches Source (supplier + price + lead time) └─ Helen promotes to ready_to_use 3. Added to project └─ Dana creates a ProjectItemSlot in the target Area └─ links a CatalogItem to the slot (primary item) └─ sets qty, finish, custom fields # Now it’s a spec line — not a picture, a data record

Notice step 3: what lands in the spec is a CatalogItem, not a CatalogFamily. That distinction is not cosmetic. A spec is a list of things that will actually be purchased. “The Aeron chair” is still a category. “Aeron, graphite, size B, 4D arms” is an order. That level of specificity is exactly what we talked about in Lesson 2 when we traced the gap between a design intent image and an actual spec line [2].

And this is where the spec-as-database idea from Lesson 1 sharpens into something concrete. The ProjectItemSlot holds a reference to the CatalogItem — not a copy of its data. If the catalog record changes (a finish gets discontinued, a price updates), the spec line knows. That reference is the foundation of the sync mechanism we’ll tear apart in Lesson 8. It’s also the mechanism that could have caught Dana’s discontinued finish before sixty chairs arrived at the warehouse in the wrong color.

Engineering note: ProjectItemSlot stores a foreign key to CatalogFamily and CatalogItem — no data is duplicated. A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper — and its rows stay connected to the catalog records that back them.
