Why It Goes Wrong — and the Tools That Catch It

Chapter 13. The discontinued finish, the tariff surprise, and the spreadsheet that can’t keep up

Two weeks before the handover package goes to Sam, Dana and Marco sit across from each other with a printout of the full project schedule. It is not a celebration. It is a post-mortem. The hotel is opening on time — barely — but they want to name every moment where it nearly didn’t.

The list is longer than either of them expected. There was the tariff hit on the bathroom vanities. There was the vendor who sent the wrong finish on sixty chairs — caught at the model room, but only because Helen printed the approved CFA record and walked in with it. And there was the discontinued headboard fabric that came within three days of sailing through to production undetected, because it was buried in row 847 of a spreadsheet and Marco’s search formula was looking at the wrong column.

Row 847 is the lesson. Not the mistake itself — that is ordinary. The lesson is that it nearly became a $40,000 mistake, and the only thing that stopped it was a manual check that could just as easily not have happened. This chapter names the pain points that make this industry hard, explains where the incumbent tools break, and maps the categories of software that exist to catch the problem before row 847 ships.

Part 1. The six things that go wrong — named and sourced

The FF&E industry has a fairly short list of recurring failure modes. The industry complains about the same six problems on every project, in every segment, at every scale. A GTM person who can name them fluently is immediately credible to anyone on the inside [1].

1 Long and unpredictable lead times
Custom casegoods and branded lighting fixtures routinely run 6–12 months from PO to delivery. The brand-mandated opening date doesn’t move. Industry advice in 2026 is to prequalify manufacturers during Design Development — not after construction drawings are complete — because “waiting for final construction drawings before engaging vendors is no longer practical” [1]. This lead-time risk is one reason Dana and Marco start expediting well before the first POs are confirmed, as we saw in Lesson 12’s model-room prep.
2 Discontinued finishes with no quick substitution
When a brand specifies a proprietary headboard or a custom lighting fixture, “substitutes are rarely approved quickly” — a minor supply hiccup becomes a major delay because the brand standard is rigid [1]. This is precisely what happened to Dana in Lesson 1: a fabric discontinued, a silent substitution, and a $40k rework. The difference between catching it at the submittal stage and catching it at the dock is the entire value of a managed specification process.
3 Tariff and freight volatility
The 2025 U.S. tariff actions hit hospitality furniture hard: a single container of Chinese upholstered furniture can now carry an effective 30–35%+ duty load (MFN base rate + Section 301 + Section 232) [2]. Owners who signed budgets before the changes “are eating costs they never anticipated.” Vietnam, the main China+1 alternative, carries a 20% baseline tariff with 40% on suspected transshipments. Ocean freight adds its own volatility on top. The practical effect: a budget fixed at spec stage can be materially wrong at PO stage — which is why the Spec vs. Budget vs. Actual gap from Lesson 8 is a live risk throughout the project.
4 Brand-standard consistency across multiple properties
A multi-property rollout for a Marriott or Hilton brand requires identical specifications across every site. Linda’s brand-standard process from Lesson 6 is not bureaucracy for its own sake: it is the only mechanism that prevents one property from receiving a slightly different laminate shade than every other property in the portfolio. Keeping specs identical across dozens of simultaneous projects while managing approved substitutions and VE changes is a coordination challenge that scales poorly with manual tools [1].
5 Version chaos in Excel across dozens of vendors
A single hotel project = thousands of line items from dozens of suppliers, each on a different schedule. When the spec lives in a spreadsheet emailed back and forth, nobody knows which version is current. Formulas break when rows are inserted. Status updates are manual. Vendor updates land in inboxes and get missed. Projects fail “because no one oversees full coordination” and contractors, designers, and procurement teams work separately [1, 3]. The moment there are three concurrent projects or 150+ items, the spreadsheet is already losing.
6 The cost of a missed opening date
A 100-room hotel at a ~$150 average daily rate loses roughly $15,000 every day it cannot open. A 10-day install delay is $150,000 in revenue that never comes back — before contractor overtime and reputational cost [1]. This is the number that makes the whole industry tense, and it is why every other pain point on this list ultimately converts back into a dollar figure tied to a date.
🤔 Predict before reading on
Marco is managing a 400-item hotel spec across three concurrent projects. He uses a shared Excel file with three team members. Think about what happens when two people edit at the same time, or when a cell reference shifts after someone inserts a row, or when a vendor responds by email and the status in the sheet never changes.

Part 2. The real incumbent — Excel, email, and PDF

Before mapping the purpose-built tools, it is worth being honest about why Excel is still the baseline for most of the industry. Competing against the incumbent requires understanding why it won [3].

✔
Why Excel wins
Free. Universal. Everyone already has it. Powerful formulas, flexible structure, no onboarding. For a single user managing a single project under ~100 items, it is genuinely good enough — and cost-benefit analysis firmly favors it.
❌
Where it breaks
Version control collapses the moment the file is emailed (“which version is current?”). No real-time collaboration. Formulas are fragile after row inserts/deletes. Status updates are manual. No vendor integration. The practical switch point: 3+ concurrent projects, 150+ items, or multiple editors [3].

Around the spreadsheet sits email — for vendor back-and-forth, approvals, and sample requests — and PDF — for final, non-editable spec sheets and spec books. The three-stack (Excel + email + PDF) is the real competitor. When a designer says “I don’t need a tool,” this is what they already have. And they are right that it works — until it doesn’t.

“Clients don’t want to see all my Excel gibberish. They want clear information — photos, easy-to-read data, and simple approvals.”

That quote — from a designer, cited by Programa — captures the first business pain that a purpose-built tool addresses [3]. The second pain is the one Marco hit: a firm working across multiple concurrent projects cannot afford to find row 847 by luck.

📊
Marco, looking at a shared Excel file that has seventeen sheets: “Why is this tab called ‘FINAL_FINAL_USE_THIS_ONE’?”
Colleague: “Because the one called ‘FINAL’ was the wrong one, and the one called ‘FINAL_ACTUAL’ was from last Tuesday.”
This is not a joke about bad naming. This is a description of how the industry manages $30M FF&E packages without version control.

Part 3. The five tool categories — what they are and who they serve

The software landscape is fragmented, and the fragmentation is structural: different parts of the FF&E process have different primary users, different workflows, and different data needs. Understanding the categories is more durable than knowing any single tool [3, 4].

Five categories. The key boundary is between the FF&E/finishes track (designer-owned) and the construction track (GC-owned). Large projects can run both simultaneously.

Part 4. Fohlio vs. Procore — the question that comes up in every sales call

Prospects and customers routinely ask whether Fohlio does what Procore does, or whether they need both. The answer is structural, not a feature comparison [3, 4].

Procore manages the building: the construction trades, the GC’s scope, the structural submittals, the RFI log, the construction schedule, the daily logs, the safety records, the punch list for construction defects. The actors are the GC, subcontractors, the architect, the structural engineer. The deliverable is a finished building shell.

Fohlio (and its peers) manage the furniture and finishes: the FF&E specification, the procurement schedule, the vendor submittals for product data and samples, the purchasing workflow, the budget tracking, the brand-standard compliance. The actors are the interior designer, the purchasing agent, the vendor, and the brand. The deliverable is a furnished and finished interior.

They overlap at two points: submittals (both processes collect product documentation from vendors) and punch lists (both close out deficiencies before handover). But on a large project both can run simultaneously — Procore tracks the GC’s submittal log for structural and mechanical work, while the FF&E spec tool tracks the designer’s submittal log for furniture and finishes. The systems talk to different people about different things [3, 4].

✋ Reconstruct the distinction
A prospect says: “We already use Procore on all our hotel projects. Why would we need anything else?” Without looking up at the table or diagram, give a two-sentence answer that names the specific thing Procore does not do for the designer’s workflow — and what the cost of that gap is.
Think about who uses Procore, what their deliverable is, and whether that person is the same as the interior designer managing the FF&E spec and vendor submittals.

Part 5. Who uses what — the practical map

The honest answer to “which tool should a firm use” depends almost entirely on project scale and primary pain point [3].

🏠
Solo / small residential (≤100 items, 1–2 people)
Excel or Google Sheets → Houzz Pro, Mydoma, Programa, or Gather as they grow.
💰
Mid-size / high-end residential (accounting-heavy)
Studio Designer, Design Manager — full accounting built in; procurement-led workflow.
🏨
Large commercial / hospitality (500–5,000+ items, brand standards, multi-project)
Fohlio, Programa (commercial tier), legacy Spexx. Dedicated FF&E procurement agencies may layer their own logistics tools on top.
🛗
General contractors / construction side
Procore, Autodesk Construction Cloud, Bluebeam — for submittals, RFIs, drawings, punch lists. This is the building track, not the FF&E track.

Part 6. Closing the loop — catching the discontinued finish before the PO ships

Marco’s near-miss with row 847 is a useful test case for what a purpose-built tool actually changes. The problem was not that Marco was careless. The problem was that the discontinuation notice from the vendor arrived by email, was logged manually on a different day, and the spec sheet — a separate Excel file — was never updated. By the time the PO was ready to issue, the spec showed the old finish and the email showed the new one, and nobody was looking at both at the same time.

Dana is watching Marco work through the vendor-scorecard update she asked for after the project closed. He pulls up the platform’s vendor activity log for the custom millwork supplier: last three projects, average lead time 14.3 weeks, one instance of a discontinued finish, one instance of a late CFA delivery. The scorecard captures exactly the pattern they lived through — the finish discontinuation from Lesson 9’s sourcing conversation, the CFA timing issue they caught before the model room.

“Next time we spec this supplier,” Dana says, “we add an expediting check at week eight.” That note goes into the supplier record. The institutional knowledge that used to live in her head now lives in a place where Marco can find it without asking her.

The mechanism a purpose-built tool provides is not magic: it is connection between things that currently live in separate places. When the spec item and the vendor record and the submittal log and the PO are all connected, a discontinuation notice from the vendor can surface against the specific items it affects — before the PO is issued, not after delivery. When the vendor scorecard is in the same system as the spec, the institutional knowledge that lives in a senior specifier’s head survives the project [5].

This is the payoff Dana was building toward since the first lesson. The $40k mistake happened because a spec stopped being true and nobody noticed. A system where discontinuation data flows to the items it affects closes that gap structurally, not by luck. It does not prevent every mistake. But it changes the question from “did anyone happen to check?” to “what does the system flag?”

The spec was right the day she sent it. The question is what happens between that day and the day the goods ship.

The tools in the FF&E Spec & Procurement category — Fohlio, Programa, and their peers — all make different choices about where exactly to intervene in that gap. Some emphasize real-time collaboration. Some emphasize brand-standard libraries. Some emphasize procurement depth. The right choice depends on where the specific firm’s biggest gap is. The GTM team’s job is to know which gap they are selling against — and to be able to connect it, clearly, to the six pain points from Part 1 [1, 3].
