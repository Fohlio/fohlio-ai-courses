The Physical Tail

Chapter 9. Where the data meets the truck

The truck pulled in at 7:40. Marco was at the Cedar & Oak loading bay with a tablet, watching the crew unload boxes. The PO said eight armchairs. Six came off the truck. And three nightstands instead of four.

A year ago that would have meant phone calls, vague supplier replies, and three weeks of uncertainty about whether the rest would ever arrive. Marco opened the receiving screen in Fohlio, found the PO, and keyed in qtyReceived against each line. The system flagged it immediately: received_partially. The supplier would be invoiced only for what arrived. The remainder stayed open, waiting for the next shipment.

Grace came around the corner with coffee. “Everything okay?” Marco nodded. “Partial. The gap is on record.” She didn’t ask what that meant. She understood the part that mattered: nothing was lost.

Phase 5 — delivery — is the moment when every month of spec work, budget negotiation, and sync checking [8] runs headlong into physical reality. Boxes. Trucks. Loading docks. This chapter is about how Fohlio models that collision — and why partial receiving matters as much as the original order [1].

What you’ll understand by the end of this lesson

  • What partial receiving means: qtyReceived vs qtyOrdered, and how PO statuses track it

  • The difference between a supplier invoice and a client invoice — and how both tie to actual in the budget [2]
  • What GrCode is and why field tracking matters at the scale of 600 identical armchairs

Part 1. From PO to shipment: one order, multiple trucks

Once a PO moves to approved, procurement is formally done: the commitment is made, the supplier is notified. But the physical goods don’t yet exist in the project as fact. They exist only as a promise — line items with a lead time attached [3].

A shipment is a record of a specific dispatch from a supplier. One PO can produce several shipments. That’s not an exception on large projects — it’s the default:

1 Staggered lead times
Armchairs ship from Italy in six months; pendant lights ship from a Texas warehouse in two weeks. One PO covers an entire guestroom, but goods arrive in waves as each item becomes ready.
2 Phased delivery by floor or site
Marriott’s 600-room renovation is broken into construction phases. Floor two takes furniture in March; floor six takes it in May. The same PO ships to multiple shipments with different dates and delivery addresses.
3 Damage replacements
Three chairs arrive dented. The supplier ships replacements. That’s a second shipment against the same PO — and the system needs to distinguish it from the first.

From the engineering side: PurchaseOrder → Shipment(s) → ShipmentItem(s). Each ShipmentItem references the PO line it belongs to, and carries the count of units in transit for that delivery.

A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper.

On the delivery phase that statement takes on a literal physical dimension. The database now needs to know what actually arrived — and what still hasn’t — down to the individual unit.

Part 2. Partial receiving: qtyReceived vs qtyOrdered

Marco isn’t standing at the loading bay with a tablet for atmosphere. His job is to record the receive: how many units of each line item actually arrived and in what condition. In Fohlio’s terms this is receiving [3].

The model is straightforward: each ShipmentItem has a qtyOrdered (how many were placed on the order) and a qtyReceived (how many were physically accepted). While qtyReceived < qtyOrdered on any line, the PO carries the status received_partially. When every line is fully received it moves to received_fully. When it’s been reconciled with an invoice and explicitly closed, it becomes closed [4].

One PO, multiple shipments. A partial receive loops back until all lines are fully closed.
🤔 Predict before you read on
PO for 8 armchairs and 4 nightstands. Delivery: 6 armchairs, 3 nightstands. What is the PO status right now? What needs to happen for it to reach closed? And what does Marco enter in qtyReceived for the armchair line?
The numbers are specific — your answer should be too.

Part 3. Warehouse, install, and GrCode — where engineers lose the thread

Accepted goods don’t teleport into position. Between “arrived at the warehouse” and “standing in room 412” there are two distinct events, and Fohlio models them as separate stages — not a single flag [4].

Warehouse — the buffer

The warehouse holds accepted goods until installation is scheduled. On a large project this gap can run for months: Marriott’s first delivery wave arrives six weeks before the first floor is ready for furnishing. The warehouse is simultaneously a physical location and a logistical entity in the system. Fohlio lets you assign items to a specific warehouse (when there are several), track movement from warehouse to site, and record the handoff date [1].

Install — the final fact

Installation is the moment a line item moves from “on the shelf” to “in place.” At this stage you record that the item is installed, the room is complete, and the final inspection can begin. It’s a separate event from receiving because the gap between the two can be significant — and both dates matter for project reporting and handover documentation [1].

GrCode — QR instead of memory

Each item on the warehouse shelf can carry a QR label — GrCode in Fohlio’s terminology. A crew member scans it on-site and immediately sees: what it is, which room it belongs to, which PO it came from, and where in the building it should go. Field tracking closes a problem that shows up constantly on large projects: the item arrived, but nobody remembers which room needs it.

📦
Crew member at the Marriott warehouse, staring at 47 identical boxes of armchairs: “Where does this one go?”

Marco (scans QR): “Room 412, second floor, chair by the window.”

Crew member (gestures at the next box): “And that one?”

Marco (scans again): “Same room, same chair — it’s a spare. Warehouse until the next install wave.”

This isn’t automation for its own sake. It’s the only way not to misplace 600 identical chairs.

Part 4. Invoices: supplier invoice, client invoice, and actual in the budget

In Lesson 6 we unpacked three budget numbers — spec total, planned, and actual. Actual was defined as “paid invoices” but we never traced where invoices come from. Now we can [2].

Fohlio works with two invoice types:

🧾
Supplier Invoice
Bill from the vendor
The supplier invoices Dana’s firm for delivered goods. Tied to a specific shipment or PO. This is what feeds actual in the budget.
📄
Client Invoice
Bill to the client
Dana’s firm invoices the client (Marriott, the Church, Grace) for services and resold goods, usually with a markup applied. A separate line in the financials.

The distinction matters for accounting: a supplier invoice is a cost to the firm; a client invoice is revenue. Fohlio stores them separately but links them through the project and its line items. By the end of a project you can see the full picture — what was spent, what was billed, what markup was realized [4].

Soft costs aren’t just goods. An invoice contains more than product lines. Delivery, packing & handling, customs duties, installation labor — these are soft costs (Lesson 6). They flow into actual through the invoice too. That’s why actual at project close consistently runs higher than spec total, even when every item was purchased at the agreed price.

✋ Explain without looking back
From Lesson 6: what is variance in the budget? Now, knowing about supplier invoices and soft costs — name two concrete scenarios where actual exceeds planned (not just “differs from” it). Why does the system need to record them separately?
One scenario is almost certainly about soft costs. The other connects to what happens when a partial receive triggers a second shipment.

Part 5. Three clients, one concept — one warehouse or three garages

Physical logistics is where the gap between the three clients is at its widest. Not conceptually — literally, in terms of square footage, staffing, and complexity of coordination.

Marriott — 600 rooms, delivery waves. The renovation runs in phases: first four floors, then six more. Each wave is its own set of shipments. The on-site warehouse can only hold one wave at a time — so Marco manages the delivery schedule the way a dispatcher manages a freight network. Too early and the warehouse overflows; too late and the install crew stands idle. Fohlio shows what has been ordered, what has been received, what is installed, and what is still in transit — broken down by each of the 600 rooms [1].

This is the scale for which batch receiving exists: Marco accepts an entire floor’s worth of goods in one operation, not item by item.

The Church of Jesus Christ — delivery by site and region. Standardized meetinghouses are built across multiple cities, sometimes multiple countries. One PO can cover several sites — buying at scale reduces per-unit cost. Brother Andersen tracks that each site receives its exact kit on schedule. Fohlio links a shipment to a specific Area or project, so one delivery record carries detail across multiple locations [4].

Grace — the garage and a free weekend. Cedar & Oak is twelve rooms. The warehouse is Grace’s garage. The install crew is Grace and her husband on a Saturday. Receiving means Grace stands at the front door and counts boxes herself. GrCode is overkill — she knows where every piece goes because there are only thirty of them. But the invoice still matters. It’s exactly how actual in her budget will show what she actually spent. The same system as Marriott — just without a procurement department [2].

Part 6. Closeout: when Phase 5 ends

Closeout is an administrative boundary: the project declares that the delivery phase is finished. Every PO has been closed — either because it reached received_fully, or because Marco has made a deliberate decision to close it (the supplier can’t ship the last two units and will issue a credit note instead). All invoices have been received and matched. The budget is locked [3].

This is not automatic. Marco explicitly closes each PO. Without that action the PO stays open and keeps contaminating budget status. Two units that will never arrive should not leave a project in a permanent state of “partially received.”

Why engineers should care about closeout. In a system without explicit closeout, open POs produce noise in every report: planned vs actual diverges for unclear reasons; budget utilization shows the wrong percentage; the final closeout document can’t be produced cleanly. An explicit closed status with a timestamp and an actor is the boundary between “in progress” and “done.” Without it, any aggregating query runs on dirty state.

After closeout, Phase 6 begins — the project handover. Install is complete, documentation is assembled. Collaboration tokens, approval workflows, and final deliverables: that’s the next chapter.

Chapter summary

Phase 5 is the most physical in the entire course. Here the data stops being data and becomes boxes on a loading dock. The system’s job is not to lose a single box.
