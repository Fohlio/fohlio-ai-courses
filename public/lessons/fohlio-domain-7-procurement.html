Lesson 7 of 10

Procurement: RFQ → Quote → PO

Chapter 7. Marco takes the spec and turns it into orders

Monday morning. Marco opens the spec and sees 847 line items. Dana closed the budget on Friday — planned is approved, variance is inside tolerance, and Linda at Marriott has signed off. The database is ready. Marco’s job now is to turn it into legally binding purchase orders before the end of the quarter. He has four weeks.

The first thing he does is not call a supplier. He groups the line items by Source.

Sixty chairs share one Source — Herman Miller through a Chicago distributor. That’s one RFQ, not sixty emails. The tile goes to a different Source. The lighting fixtures go to a third. Marco will send each of them a Request for Quote and wait for the market to answer back. Then he compares. Then he hits “Create PO.”

Between “sent the RFQ” and “hit approve,” three weeks can pass. Seven emails, two re-negotiations, and sometimes a quiet notification that a supplier has discontinued the exact finish Dana specified. That last part — we’ll come back to it at the end of this chapter.

What you’ll understand by the end of this lesson

  • How the RFQ → quote → comparison → PO cycle works in practice

  • What lead time and MOQ are — and why they determine when Marco presses “order,” not whether he does
  • Why the procurement process for Marriott, the Church, and Grace looks almost nothing alike

Part 1. Source — how Marco knows where to send the RFQ

In Lesson 3, Dana was adding products to the catalog and attaching Sources to them [3] — structured supplier records with pricing and terms. That data now belongs to Marco. A Source in the system is not a flat string like “Herman Miller, tel: +1-800-xxx.” It’s a record inside ContactCompany with its own ContactOffice and ContactPerson, tagged as source, carrying fields for price, discount, MOQ, lead time, and shipping terms [4].

Two levels of Source matter in procurement:

🏠
Family Source
Supplier at the product level
Attached to a CatalogFamily — the product line as a whole. Useful for sending one RFQ that covers the entire range, without worrying which specific variant Marco ends up ordering.
📦
Item Source
Supplier at the variant level
Attached to a specific CatalogItem (SKU). May differ from the Family Source — different distributor, different price tier, different MOQ. When both exist, Item Source takes priority [4].

When Marco sees that 60 line items share one Family Source, he sends a single RFQ to that distributor and saves a week of back-and-forth. If 5 of those 60 items carry a separate Item Source through a different distributor, they break out into their own RFQ automatically. A spec that is a database [1] makes this a query, not a manual scan.

Part 2. RFQ — asking the market a structured question

An RFQ is not an email saying “send me your price list.” It’s a structured request: specific SKUs, finishes, quantities, required delivery date, and FOB terms. The supplier receives the list and returns a quote — a response with unit price, total, lead time, and MOQ confirmation. Getting a quote back does not mean Marco is done comparing. Usually he sends the same RFQ to two or three suppliers at once [2].

Marco sent the Marriott RFQ to three suppliers simultaneously: Herman Miller Chicago, Steelcase Dallas, and a regional distributor out of Florida that Helen had recommended six months back. Five days later, three quotes landed in his inbox.

Herman Miller: $418/unit — lead time 14 weeks — MOQ 1 — FOB Origin.

Steelcase: $391/unit — lead time 10 weeks — MOQ 50 — FOB Destination.

Florida distributor: $362/unit — lead time “TBD” — MOQ 100.

Marco looked at the third row and closed it. “TBD” in the lead time field in FF&E procurement means exactly one of two things: the item is made-to-order with a real risk of six-plus months, or the seller doesn’t actually know. Either scenario is a red flag. A low unit price is worthless if the truck arrives after the ribbon-cutting.

In the data model: each quote is a record linked to an RFQ and a supplier, with fields unitPrice, totalPrice, leadTimeWeeks, moq, and incoterms. When Marco selects a quote and creates a PO from it, that quote’s status transitions to accepted [4].

Steelcase is $27/unit cheaper than Herman Miller, but FOB Destination means freight is included. The order is 300 chairs. You know freight from Chicago to New York is roughly $18/unit if you handle it yourself (as with FOB Origin). Which offer is better on total landed cost — and does the 300-unit order clear Steelcase’s MOQ? Write down your numbers before reading further.
Herman Miller total: 300 × $418 + 300 × $18 = ?    Steelcase total: 300 × $391 = ?    Steelcase MOQ = 50.

Marco compares on landed cost — unit price plus freight, duties, and handling — never on unit price alone. FOB Origin means Marco arranges shipping himself and assumes risk from the moment goods are loaded; FOB Destination means the supplier delivers to the named point and the price already includes freight [2]. Ignoring the distinction is one of the most reliable ways to make planned diverge from actual — exactly what Lesson 6 showed costs the project money.

Part 3. The PO and its five lives

A Purchase Order is a legal document: what, how many, at what price, by when, delivered where, on what terms. Unlike a quote — which is an offer — a PO is a commitment. The supplier signs. Marco signs. Money moves.

But a PO is not born final. It has a lifecycle — five statuses — and each step down the pipeline costs more to reverse than the last [4]:

PO statuses are not bureaucracy — they are the history of a financial commitment. The further right, the more it costs to reverse.
Status What happened For the engineer
draft Marco created the PO but hasn’t sent it to the supplier yet Not yet in planned. Fully editable.
open PO sent to the supplier; awaiting their confirmation Enters planned. Changes require negotiation.
approved Supplier confirmed; both parties have signed Legally binding. Lead time clock starts here.
received_partially Some line items arrived at the warehouse; the rest are still in transit actual < planned. Invoices arrive piecemeal. See Lesson 9.
received_fully Every line item has arrived; nothing is left in transit Quantities reconciled. Awaiting final invoice match before closeout. See Lesson 9.
closed All items received; all invoices paid actual = planned for this PO. Version locked.
📋
Priya: “Why not just skip straight to approved? Why do we need both draft and open?”
Marco: “Because I’ve already sent a PO and then spotted a typo in the SKU. With draft — quiet fix. Without draft — phone call to the supplier, formal cancellation request, and a very awkward conversation about why the guy who spec’d 60 chairs somehow can’t spell a part number.”

The right to be wrong gets more expensive at every status transition. draft is the only one that costs nothing.

Part 4. Lead time and MOQ — the two numbers that break schedules

Marco has one rule he could recite in his sleep: custom and imported furniture has to be ordered not when you need it, but six months before you need it. Sometimes eight [2].

Lead time is the span from PO approval to goods arriving at the warehouse. Standard in-stock furniture from a US warehouse: 2–4 weeks. Custom-fabric or custom-finish orders: 12–16 weeks. Imports from Italy or Asia: 16–26 weeks plus customs clearance. If Marriott opens rooms in October, Marco has to hit approved on custom chairs no later than April. Across three projects simultaneously, this is a chronograph, not a spreadsheet.

MOQ — Minimum Order Quantity — is the supplier’s minimum run. It’s not an arbitrary rule: you can’t manufacture three meters of custom fabric or run a dye lot for two chairs. MOQ creates the classic budget tension introduced in Lesson 6 [1]:

The MOQ trap: Grace specs 8 chairs. The supplier’s MOQ is 12. Her options are: order 12 (planned grows by 4 extra chairs), find a different supplier (new RFQ — time lost), or combine with another project (Grace has no other projects). In enterprise, this resolves by aggregation — Marriott and the Church roll multiple properties into one order and clear any MOQ threshold with margin to spare. For Grace, it’s a personal financial call every single time.

✋ Explain this before reading the next paragraph
Marco receives a quote: lead time 18 weeks, MOQ 50. Dana’s spec calls for 45 chairs. The project opens in 20 weeks. Name at least two concrete risks Marco must evaluate before hitting “approve.” Don’t read ahead — answer first.
Think about what MOQ > qty does to planned (from L6). Think about what a lead time nearly equal to the deadline means for buffer.

Two risks, both real. First: 18-week lead time with 20 weeks to opening leaves a two-week buffer. Any customs delay, any carrier hiccup, any partial hold — and the chairs aren’t there for opening day. On enterprise-scale custom orders Marco builds in a 4-week minimum buffer. Two weeks is not a buffer; it’s a gamble. Second: MOQ 50 against qty 45 means either Dana revises the spec to 50 (PO reflects the change), Marco negotiates a split-shipment exception with the supplier, or he accepts 5 extra chairs in planned. All three options require time and documentation in the PO before it goes to open.

Part 5. Three clients, three ways to procure

A spec isn’t a document — it’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper [1]. Procurement is the phase where that database turns into physical money and legal obligations. Nowhere in the lifecycle does the difference between the three segments show up more sharply.

1 Marriott — procurement team, leverage, multi-site
Marriott has a dedicated procurement department. Marco doesn’t phone one supplier — he runs a competitive RFQ to five simultaneously and waits for the market to fight over the volume. Eight properties in parallel means renegotiated volume discounts and MOQs that don’t register as a constraint. What does get complicated: each property is in a different city with a different opening date and a different delivery address. Linda won’t sign a single PO that spans the whole chain — she needs per-property cost accountability. One RFQ, many POs.
2 The Church — approved-list procurement, slow approvals
Brother Andersen’s team buys from a fixed approved-supplier list — the same brand standard introduced in Lesson 5 [3]. Marco sends RFQs only to those pre-approved suppliers. There’s no competitive bidding, but there’s also no risk of the “wrong” product slipping through. The tradeoff: PO approval requires three or four sign-off levels. A PO in draft can sit untouched for a week while it climbs the authorization chain. Marco learned to build that into the schedule from day one. Quiet budget discipline means no surprises — but also no speed.
3 Grace — no leverage, no team, every call is personal
Grace does procurement herself — there is no Marco. She opens the spec in Fohlio, finds the Source attached to each item, and emails the supplier. The RFQ is a Gmail draft. The quote is the reply. She creates the PO in Fohlio because it’s the only way she has to track what’s been ordered and what hasn’t. MOQ 50 at qty 12 isn’t just an accounting line for Grace — it’s the question of whether she has the cash for 38 extra chairs she didn’t plan for.

Part 6. Something happens between “chose” and “bought”

Marco closes the quote-comparison table on Friday afternoon. Steelcase won the Marriott chair order — best landed cost, lead time fits the schedule. He creates the PO in draft. He needs three days of final verification before moving it to open: one last pass over SKUs and finishes, a confirmation that the delivery address is correct for the first property, a check that the total matches the approved planned figure from Lesson 6 [1].

Monday morning. He opens the PO.

One line item — a chair with a specific fabric SKU — has a yellow flag on it. A marker that wasn’t there Friday.

End hook: between Friday and Monday, something changed in the catalog. The supplier updated information on that item — possibly the exact finish Dana specified. The yellow flag is a signal from the sync engine: this line item in the spec no longer matches the current data in the catalog. This is not a coincidence. Those “three days in draft” are precisely the window that cost Dana $40k eighteen months ago: enough time passed between “we chose this” and “we hit approve” for reality to shift while the spec stayed the same.

What to do with the yellow flag — pull, push, or replace — is Lesson 8.

Chapter summary

Marco took the spec — a database [1] — and began converting it into commitments. RFQ and quotes are market intelligence. A PO is an obligation. The statuses are the audit trail of that obligation, from a fixable draft all the way to a closed financial record.

📞
Source / Supplier
Structured supplier record with price, MOQ, lead time. Family level or Item level — Item takes priority.
📨
RFQ → Quote
Structured price request to one or more suppliers. Compare on landed cost, not unit price. Selected quote becomes the PO basis.
📝
PO + 5 statuses
draft → open → approved → received_partially → closed. Each transition is harder to reverse than the last.
⏱
Lead time + MOQ
Lead time dictates when you must order, not whether. MOQ creates budget pressure that hits SMBs hardest.
A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper. Procurement is the first moment that database becomes money — and the first moment it can go stale.

Coming up in Lesson 8: the yellow flag on Marco’s Monday PO isn’t a bug. It’s the sync engine working. Over the three days that PO sat in draft, something in the catalog changed.     ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    Correct order: RFQ → quote → PO draft → PO open → PO approved → PO received_partially → PO closed (with shipment running in parallel from approved onward). Transition conditions: RFQ → quote: supplier responds with price and lead time. Quote → PO draft: Marco selects a quote and creates a draft PO from it. Draft → open: Marco has verified SKUs, quantities, and address; sends to supplier. Open → approved: both parties sign. Approved → received_partially: first partial shipment arrives at the warehouse. Received_partially → closed: all line items received and all invoices paid. A pass means getting the order right and giving a condition that references something external — not just “Marco decides.”

    • How does switching Source affect specTotal and planned (L6)?
    • Does switching Source require a new RFQ (L3 + L7)?
    • Does lead time 8 weeks fit a 10-week deadline — and is the buffer enough?
    • Is ordering 15 units at qty 10 worth it (L6 MOQ trap)?
    ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    specTotal drops $400 (10 × $40). Planned depends on MOQ: if Grace orders exactly 10, she needs a supplier willing to go below MOQ — unlikely; if qty becomes 15, planned rises despite the cheaper unit price. New Source = new RFQ required: without a quote from the new supplier, there is no legal basis for a PO. Lead time 8 weeks against a 10-week deadline leaves a 2-week buffer — with any logistics hiccup (port delay, customs hold), this is riskier than the original 4-week supplier; the $40/unit saving doesn’t offset a blown opening date. MOQ 15 at qty 10: the 5 extra chairs cost money Grace likely doesn’t have a use for; unless she truly wants the savings and has storage, the original supplier with MOQ 1 is the safer call. A pass requires addressing all four questions with the tradeoffs named.
