Chapter 10. The finish: one project delivered, and the chair arrived

Dana opens a project and the cursor blinks in a familiar field — the project name, only this time it isn’t empty. It reads: Cedar & Oak — Status: Delivered. Outside, it’s an ordinary Tuesday morning. Inside Dana, it’s the thing she’d call the absence of the chill.

In the lobby of Grace’s boutique inn, twelve armchairs are standing in exactly the right finish. Grace sent a photo from her phone twenty minutes ago. Dana looks at the screen and lets the moment sit for exactly three seconds before she moves to the next project.

Nine chapters ago, Dana sat at that same blank “Project name” field with a cold mug of coffee and the memory of sixty wrong-color armchairs costing forty thousand dollars in rework [1]. Today the sync engine caught the discontinued finish before Marco sent the PO — we covered how in Lesson 8. This chapter is the close: the client signs, the deliverables go out, the project is handed over. We’ll trace exactly how — and what it looks like for three very different clients.

Part 1. Phase 6 — what “delivered” actually means

After Lesson 9, the chair is physically in the lobby. But “project delivered” is not a physical fact — it’s a contractual one. It happens the moment the client confirms they received what was specified, in the right quantity and condition. Until that confirmation, money may not clear and claims are still open [1].

Phase 6 is the final leg of the six-phase conveyor we introduced in Lesson 1. Installation and final inspection are technically the end of Phase 5; handover proper is: preparing deliverables, granting controlled client access, closing the approval chain, and archiving. The word “handover” maps cleanly to three actions in Fohlio: generate the spec-book, send the collaboration link, collect the approval [2].

Dana opens the Cedar & Oak project for the third time that morning. In the top-right corner: the Share button. She first pressed it six weeks ago, when she gave Grace a view of the spec. Grace opened it, looked at the 30-row table, got nervous, and closed it. Today Dana opens it again — this time for Grace to sign.

Collaboration: the Fohlio model for giving external participants (clients, vendors) scoped access to a project or part of a project, without a full Workspace account. Two modes: share (read + comment) and approval_request (explicit approve / reject). The field that distinguishes them: logicType on the Collaboration entity [3].

The difference between share and approval_request is small in code and large in meaning. share says “you can see it.” approval_request says “you can see it, and I need your signature.” In handover, only the second one closes the loop.

Part 2. Collaboration: tokens, links, comments

An external participant — client or vendor — has no Workspace account. They get a tokenized link that gives them exactly as much access as the Collaboration record specifies, and nothing beyond it [3]. This is not security theater: Marriott is genuinely adamant that Linda should never accidentally see the studio’s budget or another firm’s project on the same platform.

Internal roles vs. external collaborations. The token sets the scope — the client doesn’t “log in” to the system; they open a specific view.

Comments in a Collaboration aren’t chat. They are slot-anchored notes that stay in the project history. When Linda writes “this sofa isn’t in our approved catalog” — as we saw in Lesson 5 when we traced how brand standards block non-compliant items — that comment is visible to Dana directly in context of that specific slot, not buried in an email thread [2]. For Marriott, with decisions flowing across hundreds of items, that context anchoring is not convenience; it is the difference between a decision that can be audited and one that cannot.

🤔 Predict before reading on
Grace has no procurement team and no brand manager. She opens a tokenized link and approves it herself. Her approval_request chain has exactly one person. Now say out loud or write down: why does it matter that this is approval_request and not just share — even for a chain of one?
Hint: think about money and disputes. What happens six weeks later when a client says “I never agreed to that finish”?

Part 3. Roles and permissions — not decoration

In Lesson 1 we introduced roles in one sentence: “Dana edits the spec, Marco handles procurement, the client sees only their own.” Over nine chapters we watched that play out. Here is the full picture.

System roles determine what is visible at all. Custom roles are sets of permission keys that enable or disable specific actions within that role [3].

Role Who this is in the studio What they can / cannot do
Owner Helen (studio founder) Everything, including billing, deleting the Workspace, reassigning roles.
Admin Senior manager Helen trusts with operations Everything except billing and Workspace deletion.
Member Dana, Marco, Priya — each with their own permission key set Scoped by keys: projects_spec, catalog_edit, procurement_*, and others.
Collaborator External participant (client or vendor) via token Only what the token scope defines: project / area / slot, logicType, expiry.

Custom roles are templates on top of Member. Specifier is Dana’s role: she can edit the spec, add to the catalog, and cannot see procurement pricing. Procurement is Marco’s: he manages RFQ, quote, PO — he cannot directly edit the spec. Vendor is an external supplier given token access to specific items for pricing — nothing else visible. The boundary between Specifier and Procurement is not a formality. It records who owns a decision, not just who clicked a button [3].

🔑
Priya: “Why do we need all these roles? Let’s just give everyone Admin and stop arguing about it.”
Marco (without looking up from the RFQ table): “Sure. Next time Linda accidentally deletes three rooms from our spec, you can explain it to Helen.”
Permissions aren’t bureaucracy. They are the boundary between “who can press the button” and “who is accountable for what happened.”

Part 4. Three clients — three approval chains

One approval_request mechanism. Three completely different scenarios. This is the cleanest demonstration of the rule from Lesson 1: one feature has to work for all three.

Marriott: many stakeholders, brand approval

Linda is the brand manager, but she is not the last signature. A typical Marriott chain: Dana sends an approval_request to Linda on the spec-book for a room type. Linda annotates it with comments — “this chair isn’t from the approved catalog,” exactly the kind of block we covered in Lesson 5 when we traced brand standards in practice. Then the regional operations director confirms the budget. Without that second signature, the PO doesn’t go out. Marco knows this, so he initiates the approval_request in parallel with the RFQ — not after it. If he waits for approval before sending the RFQ, lead time slips by weeks [1]. That timing discipline was the core of Lesson 7.

The Church: long chain, budget approval

Brother Andersen doesn’t decide alone. The institutional process runs through a local facilities coordinator, then a regional facilities manager, then a financial committee if the total exceeds a threshold. The spec sits in “pending approval” the entire time. Brother Andersen doesn’t rush — he is stewarding donated funds, and every step needs an audit trail [4]. For us, the engineering implication is concrete: in the institutional segment, an approval_request can sit open for weeks and that is correct behavior. The system should not time it out or send aggressive reminders.

Grace: chain of one

Grace is her own client. She gets a tokenized link for the Cedar & Oak spec-book, opens it on her phone — this is a mobile-first scenario — looks at the photos of the chairs, sees the total, and taps “Approve.” Done. A chain of one person is legally the same signature as the Church’s financial committee. The difference is only the time it takes [4].

Part 5. Deliverables — spec-books, proposals, reports

Phase 6 is not only the client signature. It is the handover of documentation. In Dana’s world “documentation” is a set of files: the spec-book (a paginated list of all items with photos and finishes), proposals (budget summaries and pricing for client approval), takeoff reports (BOM-level quantity breakdowns for the installer).

The key point for engineers: none of these are Word documents that someone types by hand. They are generated from the spec data [2]. This is the image-anchor from Lesson 1 returning one final time:

A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper.

For Marriott, the spec-book is evidence of brand-standard compliance: every item links to its entry in the approved catalog [5]. For the Church, it is the closeout documentation that satisfies the financial committee’s audit requirement [4]. For Grace, it is a page of photos and prices she can show her business partner before she taps “Approve.” Three segments, same data, different representations [6].

📑
Spec-book
Paginated item list with photos, finishes, suppliers. Generated from the spec. Brand compliance evidence for Marriott.
📊
Proposal
Budget summary for client sign-off: spec total, item status, markup. A snapshot of the spec at the moment of approval.
📋
Takeoff / Report
BOM-level quantity breakdown. Needed by the installer during installation and final inspection.

Part 6. One chair, three clients — the full journey

This is the capstone of the capstone. We follow one specific object — the chair that haunted this whole course — from design intent to client signature, across all three clients. The structure of the journey is the same. The details are completely different. That difference is what determines how we design features.

Phase Marriott (Linda) The Church (Brother Andersen) Cedar & Oak (Grace)
Intent (L2) Chair is already in the brand book. Linda sends the moodboard with a part number attached. Fixed kit from the previous project: this chair was approved three years ago. Grace sent a Pinterest screenshot: “something like this but warmer.” Priya finds the actual SKU.
Spec (L3, L4) 600 chairs by room type. Area = each type. Schedule = Seating. Repeatability is the whole point. Fixed kit: one chair variant, fixed quantity per floor plan. No variations allowed. 12 chairs, one type, one Area. A 30-row spec. Priya fills it in half a day.
Brand standard (L5) Linda checks: is this chair in the approved catalog? Yes → proceed. No → full stop, comment in Collaboration. Chair is from the Church’s fixed kit. The standard is rigid; no derivations are permitted. No standard. Grace trusts Dana’s judgment and the price sheet.
Budget (L6) Negotiated markup per Marriott contract. Line-item budget by room type, across properties. Chair cost × number of facilities. Variance against the approved budget is an audit requirement. Grace tracks total herself; Dana shows spec total directly in the spec. No surprises.
Procurement (L7) RFQ to three suppliers. Marco compares quotes. MOQ is not a problem: 600 chairs gives leverage. Supplier from the previous project. Price is known; PO is nearly a template. One supplier. MOQ = 6, order = 12. Grace handles this herself.
Sync (L8) Engine flags: chair finish discontinued. Sync state = broken (orphaned reference). Marco sees it before the PO goes out. Fixed kit updated — supplier changed the SKU. Pull proposes updating across all facilities projects. The exact $40k failure scenario: sync catches the finish substitution before the order. Arc closes here.
Delivery (L9) Shipments in waves of 200 chairs. Partial receiving. On-site warehouse per property. Delivery by region and facility. GR code on every batch. Everything arrives in one day. Grace receives it herself, stores in the garage.
Handover (L10) approval_request to Linda then regional director. Spec-book as brand compliance proof. approval_request: local coordinator → regional → financial committee. Proposal for audit file. approval_request to Grace. One tap on her phone. Photo in WhatsApp. Done.

The takeaway from this table: the mechanism is one — approval_request, tokenized link, signature. But the number of participants, the time horizon, and the stakes are completely different. A feature that works cleanly for Grace (fast, mobile, one tap) must also work for Marriott (multi-level, comment-threaded, auditable history). This is the discipline from Lesson 1: when you argue about a ticket’s priority, ask “is this for Linda, for Brother Andersen, or for Grace?”

  2. Course-wide self-check. Answer these without opening any lesson (give yourself five minutes):
    • (a) What is SyncStateCache and why is it transient rather than persisted?
    • (b) What is the difference between spec total, planned, and actual? What does variance measure?
    • (c) Why does the Collaborator role have no access to other Workspace projects?
    • (d) Name two scenarios in which sync state becomes broken.
    ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    (a) SyncStateCache is the computed link state between a spec item and its catalog source: new / ready / updates / broken. It is transient because it reflects the current state of the catalog, which can change at any moment; a persisted value would go stale immediately after any catalog edit. Better to compute on demand than to store a lie. (b) Spec total = sum of what was specified. Planned = sum of issued POs (what was ordered). Actual = sum of paid invoices (what was paid). Variance = planned − specTotal (positive means over the approved spec, negative means saved) — the same definition as Lesson 6. (c) The Workspace is the multi-tenant isolation boundary. A Collaborator accesses the system through a token that is scoped to a specific project, area, or slot — not through an authenticated account. Outside the token’s scope, nothing is visible. (d) Broken: (1) orphaned reference — the catalog item the spec item pointed to has been deleted; (2) componentsDiverged — the BOM (Parts) in the catalog has changed in a way that cannot be auto-merged with the project-level parts, requiring a manual decision.
