The Fohlio Domain for Engineers

Chapter 1. An empty project and 60 chairs in the wrong finish

7:14 a.m. Dana Reyes sits in front of a blank project with a mug of coffee that went cold an hour ago. The cursor blinks in the “Project name” field. Outside, it’s an ordinary Tuesday morning at a twenty-person design studio. Inside Dana, it’s the thing she calls “the chill before the spec.”

Eighteen months ago she ignored that chill. She filled out the specification for a hotel lobby, sent it to procurement, and left for vacation. Six weeks later, 60 armchairs arrived at the warehouse. The upholstery was wrong. The manufacturer had quietly discontinued that exact shade while the chairs sat in the order queue, and substituted the “closest match.” Nobody caught it. The rework bill came to about forty thousand dollars. The schedule slipped by a month. Ever since, Dana opens every new project as if it could cost her forty thousand dollars all over again.

Today there isn’t one project on her desk — there are three. And all three are completely different people who want completely different things. Dana is the person we’ll follow for the next ten lessons. Not because she’s a hero. Because her job is the domain we build software for.

What you’ll understand by the end of this lesson

After this chapter you’ll be able to:

  • Explain what FF&E is — and why it’s a class of problems, not just “furniture”
  • Name the six phases of a project’s lifecycle, from intent to handover
  • Tell apart Fohlio’s three customer types and understand why they think differently
  • See the entities behind the UI (Project, Workspace, roles)
  • Pin down which phase the $40k mistake actually happened in — and why the domain exists to catch it

Part 1. Who Dana is, and what’s on her desk

Dana is a senior FF&E specifier. Her job: decide which objects go into a building, pick them from suppliers, do the math on the money, and shepherd them to the moment they physically stand in their places. She is not an interior designer in the “I pick curtain colors to match a mood” sense. She’s the person who turns a pretty picture into a list of a thousand line items you can actually buy.

The same people will show up around her throughout this course. Learn them now — they come back in every chapter.

And then there are the clients — and this is where it gets interesting for us. But before the clients, we need to settle one word that gets said every five minutes in this domain.

Part 2. What FF&E is (and why it isn’t a trip to IKEA)

FF&E stands for Furniture, Fixtures & Equipment. Bluntly: it’s everything in a building you could (in theory) carry out without breaking the walls. Chairs, tables, lighting, rugs, mirrors, bar counters, lamps, door hardware, the dishes in the hotel restaurant. Walls, slabs, and ductwork are not FF&E — that’s the building’s “shell” [2].

Sounds like a shopping list. That’s the trap. A shopping list for an apartment is twenty rows. Here’s the scale of a real project.

🤔 Guess before you read on
A 600-room Marriott hotel. How many distinct line items are in its FF&E specification, and how many suppliers deliver them? Say a number out loud, or write it down.
Being wrong here is fine — your brain just set a hook for the real number to land on.

A typical hospitality project runs 500 to 5,000+ line items, 30 to 100+ suppliers, and a procurement cycle of 12–24 months. Custom and imported pieces ship in six months or more, so procurement runs in parallel with construction, not after it [3]. Each line item isn’t “a chair.” It’s a chair of a specific model, in a specific finish, in the right quantity, from a chosen supplier, at an agreed price, with a lead time and a status of “ordered / in transit / received.” Multiply by three thousand rows.

That’s why Dana’s mistake cost $40k, not forty dollars. On a twenty-row shopping list you catch the wrong color with your eyes. On a three-thousand-row spec, nobody catches it — until it shows up at the warehouse.

A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper.

That idea is the backbone of the whole course. Hold on to it. Everything we build in Fohlio exists for one reason: to keep each of those thousands of rows true all the way from intent to the loading dock.

📄
Priya: “Why can’t we just keep it all in Excel? Everyone does.”
Dana: “Open the project folder.”
*Priya opens it. Inside: spec_FINAL.xlsx, spec_FINAL_v2.xlsx, spec_FINAL_USE_THIS.xlsx, spec_FINAL_USE_THIS_real.xlsx.*
Excel doesn’t lose data. It loses the answer to “which version is true right now” — and that answer is exactly what we get paid for.

Part 3. Six phases: the journey of one line item

Behind the tidy word “project” hides a conveyor of six phases. Any line item — that one chair — passes through them in order. Understanding these phases matters more than knowing any single product screen: every later chapter of this course lives inside one of them [1].

Every chapter (Lessons 2–10) takes one of these phases apart from the inside.

Notice the arrow that loops back from phase 3 to phase 4 — not in a straight line, but across the full width. Real life works the same way: weeks of approvals sit between “budget is set” and “hit buy.” And the chair from phase 2 can quietly “go stale” by phase 4 — the manufacturer drops the finish while the spec waits for sign-off. That exact gap in time is what cost Dana $40k. Lesson 8 is entirely about how Fohlio closes it.

Part 4. Three clients, one concept

Here’s the main reason you can’t learn the domain “in general”: Fohlio doesn’t have one customer, it has several very different ones. They open the same spec screen with opposite goals. Keep only one type in your head and you’ll design a feature that breaks for the other two.

Dana has exactly three on her desk right now, one from each world. Meet them — we’ll come back to them in every chapter in a “Three clients” beat.

Client Who they are How they think — and their core job (JTBD)
Marriott
enterprise, hospitality		 A hotel chain. A 600-room refresh plus rolling the standard out across dozens of properties. Face of it: Linda, brand manager. “Make every hotel in the chain look identical.” The brand book outranks personal taste. JTBD: replicate an approved standard with zero drift and control it across hundreds of line items.
The Church of Jesus Christ
enterprise, institutional		 An institutional owner. A batch of standardized meetinghouses built to a fixed standard. Face of it: Brother Andersen, facilities & purchasing. “Take one proven design and repeat it thousands of times, carefully with the money.” They steward donated funds, so budget discipline is strict and the horizon is years. JTBD: replicate predictably and keep cost under control.
Cedar & Oak
SMB, small business		 A 12-room boutique inn. The owner, Grace, is her own designer, buyer, and bookkeeper. “I have no time, no procurement team, and I’m scared of going over budget.” Decides fast, on the fly. JTBD: get the project done solo without losing money or dropping a thread.

Key idea: Marriott is obsessed with consistency, the Church with budget discipline and replication, Grace with speed and simply not dropping a single thread. One spec feature has to work for all three. When you argue about priorities in a ticket, ask yourself: “is this for Linda, for Brother Andersen, or for Grace?”

Part 5. What we’re actually modeling

Now — from Dana’s world to our entities. When Dana hits “create project,” a tree of objects is born under the hood — the thing we build. Here’s the top level, just enough to keep the map in your head; the details come in later chapters [4].

Workspace ← the firm’s account (Dana’s studio). Data isolation boundary ├── Members + Roles ← who’s inside and what they may do (Dana, Marco, Priya, Helen) ├── Catalog ← the firm’s shared product library (Lesson 3) └── Projects ← three projects: Marriott, the Church, Cedar & Oak └── Project ← the container: rooms, spec, budget, orders ├── Areas ← rooms / zones (lobby, guest room, restaurant) └── Items ← the thousands of spec line items

Three things worth taking away from this tree right now:

🏢
Workspace
The multi-tenant boundary. One firm’s data is invisible to another. Billing lives here too.
🔑
Roles
Dana edits the spec, Marco the procurement, the client sees only their own. Permissions aren’t decoration.
📦
Project
The container for everything: rooms, line items, budget, orders. All work starts here.

And the one mental shift this whole chapter is written for: a Project is not “a folder of documents.” It’s a live database where every line item knows its product, supplier, price, lead time, and status. That’s the idea from Part 2 again. A document shows the past. A database answers “what’s true right now” — which is exactly where Dana got burned.
