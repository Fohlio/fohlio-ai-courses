Three Prices for the Same Chair

Chapter 7. Money, Part 1 — how retail, trade, and fee actually stack up, and who keeps what

Tomás Vega slides a price list across the table. Marco glances at it and smiles, which is the professional version of rolling his eyes. The number on the page — $1,240 per unit for the lounge chair — is the retail price, which is roughly what a member of the public would pay if they walked into a showroom and pointed at it. Tomás knows Marco has no intention of paying that. Marco knows Tomás knows. They both know the real conversation is about the trade price.

The trade price for this chair, for a buyer like Marco’s firm, is somewhere around $620 — fifty percent off list. That is not a promotional discount or a favor. It is the price that professional purchasing firms pay because they are essentially operating as a wholesale channel for the manufacturer. They order in volume, they specify in advance, they handle client management, they take on a share of the risk. The manufacturer prices accordingly.

Which brings up the question the owner’s rep Sam will ask at the budget meeting next Thursday: if the chair is $620 to Marco, why does it show up in the project budget at $890?

Part 1. Two prices before you even start negotiating

Almost every product in the contract-furniture and hospitality-furnishing world has two prices: the retail price (also called the list price or MSRP) and the trade price (also called the net price). They are not the same number, they do not move together, and understanding the gap between them is the first prerequisite for understanding anything else about how money flows in FF&E.

The retail price

The retail price is what the public pays. It is the number on the showroom tag. It is also, in the contract world, largely theatrical — because almost nobody in a professional hospitality project pays it. It exists as a reference point, a baseline, and occasionally as a marketing tool (discounts are easier to communicate when there is a number to discount from).

The trade price

The trade price is available to verified design professionals and purchasing firms through a manufacturer’s trade program. Getting access requires a business registration, a tax ID or resale certificate, and proof of active professional practice — typically a portfolio or business website. The application takes one to two weeks per brand. Once approved, the firm buys at a wholesale-equivalent rate that is typically 20 to 60% below retail, depending on the manufacturer [1]:

🏪
Retail brands with trade program
10–20% off retail. Names available in consumer showrooms that also serve the trade.
🏭
Trade-only manufacturers
30–50% off list. Not available in consumer retail; only sold through approved design professionals.
🌍
European / factory-direct
40–60% off retail. Typically accessed through specialized importers or direct trade accounts.

Why does this gap exist? Because the manufacturer is using designers and purchasing firms as a distribution channel. The designer specifies the product into a project; the purchasing firm orders it in volume and manages delivery. The manufacturer saves on retail infrastructure, showroom costs, and direct sales effort. The trade discount is the manufacturer’s cost of using that channel [1].

The keystone markup

The classic rule of thumb in furniture retail is keystone pricing: the retailer doubles the wholesale cost to set the retail price. A 100% markup means the retailer’s gross margin is 50% — they paid $500 for something they sell at $1,000 [2].

Keystone is a starting point, not a law. Real furniture markups range from 20% to 400% over wholesale depending on the item, the brand, the service burden, and whether the sale includes white-glove delivery, warranty, and damage liability. A high-end custom lounge chair with a 12-week lead time and complex COM upholstery carries a different margin economics than a commodity side table. But if you are in a negotiation and someone says “keystone”, now you know: they mean the retail price is roughly twice the trade price, and they are working backward from that [2].

Part 2. The three ways a designer earns on product

The $270 gap in the question above is not a hidden fee. It is the fee — or the markup — depending on which model the designer or purchasing firm uses. There are three main models, and they are all legitimate and all common [3]:

Model Mechanic Typical number Client sees net price?
Retail / spread Buy at trade (net), sell at or near list price, keep the spread Net × 2–3.5×; ASID survey: markup 30–50% above trade No — client sees retail only
Cost-plus / markup Quote the net cost plus a stated percentage 20–35% markup over trade (30–35%+ in major markets) Yes — both numbers disclosed
Hybrid Design fee for creative work; product billed separately at cost-plus or retail depending on item Varies by engagement structure Partial — depends on the item

In the cost-plus model, a worked example from an actual designer’s published rate card: $8,999 retail − 40% designer discount = $5,399 net × 1.35 markup = $7,289 client price [3]. The client pays less than retail, the designer earns on the product, and the math is visible to everyone. That transparency is the whole point of cost-plus — it is the response to clients who ask “but what did you pay for it?”

BIID research found that roughly 35% of UK designers work as “principal” — they buy at trade and resell at retail, taking full legal liability for the goods — while another 35% blend models; about 68% do not disclose the trade price to clients at all [3]. In residential design, the spread model is the norm. In hospitality procurement, the norm is different — and it is worth understanding why.

Part 3. The fiduciary model — how Marco actually gets paid

Marco’s firm is not a design firm. It is an FF&E purchasing agent — specifically, a fiduciary purchasing agent, which means something precise in the hospitality world: the agent works for the owner, passes all trade discounts to the client, and is compensated only by a disclosed fee. The trade discount is not the agent’s income — it belongs to the owner [4].

This is the model Benjamin West describes as its operating structure, and it is increasingly the hospitality standard. The logic is clean: the owner is trusting the purchasing agent to make decisions on their behalf — vendor selection, pricing negotiations, quality oversight. If the agent also keeps a portion of every discount they negotiate, their incentive is misaligned. A fiduciary agent has no reason to prefer any vendor over another for financial reasons; their only income is the disclosed fee.

Sam (the owner’s rep) asked Marco the question directly at the project kickoff: “How do you make money?” Marco answered in one sentence: “We charge 4% of the FF&E procurement value. On a €3M package, that is €120,000. Every trade discount we negotiate comes off your budget, not into our pocket.” Sam wrote the number down. Then he asked who verified that the discounts actually moved. Marco said: “Your auditor can review every PO and every supplier invoice. That’s what the disclosed part means.”

Sam hired them.

The other purchasing-agent models

The fiduciary/disclosed-fee model is not the only one. Two others are common [4]:

📊
Management fee
Agent model, with a percentage
The agent charges 10–20% of FF&E trade value as a management fee; discounts still pass to the client. The BIID recommends 15% as the standard for commercial projects. Higher rate than the fiduciary model but covers more active management on smaller or more complex projects.
💰
Principal / markup
Buy and resell
The firm buys at trade (20–40% below retail) and resells at or near retail, keeping the spread. Industry average markup ~35% (range 30–50%). More common in residential design than hospitality. The firm takes on full retailer legal liability for the goods.

There is also a tiered percentage structure: for example, 8% on the first £50,000 of FF&E value, then 4% above that. This rewards agents for bringing larger projects (lower rate on volume) while covering the fixed overhead on smaller ones [4].

Part 4. Tomás — the invisible commission

Back to Tomás Vega. He is a manufacturer’s rep — an independent salesperson who carries multiple non-competing furniture lines across a defined territory. He is not employed by any one manufacturer. He earns on commission.

Here is the structural fact that surprises most people: Tomás is paid by the manufacturer, not by the buyer. Marco’s firm does not write Tomás a check. The manufacturer pays Tomás a commission — typically 5 to 15% of the sale value depending on the product and market [5] — after the order ships. From Marco’s perspective, Tomás’s commission is invisible: it does not appear on the invoice, it does not change the trade price, and Marco has no direct obligation to it. It is the manufacturer’s cost of using an outside sales force instead of salaried staff.

🤝
Marco: “So Tomás, if I bring this project to you and it’s three hundred chairs, what are you going to do for me?”
Tomás: “I’ll give you a great price, a fast lead time, a factory visit, and I will personally call the plant manager every week until the order ships.”
Marco: “And what does the manufacturer think about all that personal attention?”
Tomás: “They’re paying for it.”
The rep model is elegant: the manufacturer gets a motivated salesperson who only earns if they close. Marco gets a point of contact with a factory relationship. Nobody tells Dana any of this unless she asks.

Tomás’s core job is not the transaction — it is getting the product specified. If Dana writes the chair into the spec at the DD phase (the design development stage we covered in Lesson 4), the manufacturer’s rep has effectively won the sale before a quote was ever issued. This is why Tomás spends considerable time building relationships with design firms, attending showrooms and trade shows like HD Expo and BDNY, and leaving product samples with studios. The specification is the revenue event; the purchase order is just the paperwork [5].

Part 5. Volume rebates and what large buyers actually pay

The trade price is not the floor. For large orders, manufacturers offer additional reductions — either negotiated at the time of the order or paid back as volume rebates at the end of a period once purchasing thresholds are met.

Volume rebates are returned as cash, credit, or future discounts after the buyer has hit a spend threshold with that manufacturer. They typically deliver 3 to 15% additional savings on purchasing volume, and they are specifically designed to protect the list price (the reward is earned only after volume is achieved, so the manufacturer doesn’t have to publicly lower prices) [6].

For large hospitality purchasing firms that are placing orders across dozens of properties at the same brand flag — the same chair across two hundred hotels in a brand refresh cycle — volume rebates become a meaningful part of the economics. The firm’s ability to aggregate spend across a client portfolio and earn rebates is part of the case for hiring a purchasing agent rather than managing procurement property by property. This is the parallel to what we saw in Lesson 6: brand standards create not just compliance requirements but also procurement scale — because if every property in a flag needs the same chair, that is a consolidated order, which is a volume negotiation, which is a rebate [6].

On top of volume rebates, large orders often attract a separate project discount of 5 to 15% off standard trade pricing — but only if you ask for it, and only if you consolidate enough spec around fewer vendors to create real volume for that manufacturer [1]. The industry phrase for this is “earned discount,” and it disappears the moment the project is fragmented across too many suppliers.

Part 7. Why this matters beyond the math

Understanding the pricing structure is not a finance exercise. It changes the conversation at every level of the value chain, and it is the kind of knowledge that distinguishes a GTM team that can speak fluently to customers from one that cannot.

Three practical implications:

For the owner: the question is not “what is the trade price?” — it is “which model am I on, and does my agent’s income depend on which supplier they choose?” A fiduciary model answers that question structurally. A principal model doesn’t, and the owner should ask the question explicitly.

For the designer: the choice of pricing model affects the client relationship fundamentally. A cost-plus model builds trust through transparency; a spread model maximizes income on popular items but creates adversarial dynamics the moment the client asks about it. Most experienced studios use a hybrid — cost-plus on items where they are adding real specification value, retail or near-retail on commodity goods [3].

For the whole chain: the brand-standard world (Lesson 6) and the pricing world (this lesson) intersect precisely at the approved-supplier list. When a brand mandates specific suppliers, it constrains the purchasing agent’s ability to negotiate on price — there is no competitive bidding if there is only one approved vendor. This is a structural tension that no pricing model resolves; it is a cost of brand consistency, and it goes directly to the PIP budget calculations owners have to make when they receive the renovation mandate.

Coming up in Lesson 8: the three prices (retail, trade, and net-landed) are only the product price. There is a second, parallel cost stack that shows up on every project invoice and almost never in the early budget: freight, duties, warehousing, receiving, installation, taxes, and contingency — together adding 15 to 35% on top of the product price. We follow the budget from the first spec estimate to the actual invoiced cost, and look at where the gaps open and why.

Required (for everyone):

  1. Trace the money through a worked example. A purchasing agent buys a hotel guestroom lounge chair at a trade price of $480 (40% off $800 list). The agent is on a cost-plus model, charging a 30% markup to the client. (a) What does the client pay? (b) How much does the agent earn per unit? (c) If the manufacturer’s rep earns a 10% commission from the manufacturer on the trade price, what is the rep’s commission per unit, and does the client’s price change because of it? Now restate the same scenario but with the agent on a fiduciary model charging a 4% flat fee on $200,000 total FF&E value. Compare who ends up ahead in each scenario: the agent, or the owner?
    ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    Cost-plus scenario: (a) Client pays $480 × 1.30 = $624. (b) Agent earns $624 − $480 = $144 per unit. (c) Rep commission = 10% × $480 = $48, paid by the manufacturer — does not appear on the client’s invoice; client price does not change because of it. Fiduciary scenario: agent charges 4% × $200,000 = $8,000 total for the whole package. If there are 200 chairs in the package, that is $40/unit. Client pays $480/unit + $40/unit fee = $520/unit total effective cost. On 200 chairs: cost-plus model total = 200 × $624 = $124,800; fiduciary total = 200 × $480 + $8,000 = $104,000. Owner is ahead by $20,800 in the fiduciary model. Agent earns $8,000 total vs $28,800 in the cost-plus model. Key insight: the fiduciary model consistently favors the owner on large volumes; the cost-plus model is agent-favorable at the same markup rate.
  2. Cumulative interleave — connect L6 and L7. A mid-size hotel receives a PIP mandating replacement of guestroom lounge seating with a specific chair from a single approved supplier. The brand has not approved any alternates. Answer: (a) From L6, what constraint does the approved-supplier list create for the purchasing agent’s ability to negotiate price? (b) From L7, how does the rep commission model (Tomás) interact with an approved-supplier situation — does Tomás have more or less leverage than on a competitively bid spec? (c) If you were the owner reviewing this, which purchasing-agent compensation model (fiduciary vs. cost-plus vs. management fee) would you prefer, and why does the answer depend on the approved-supplier constraint from L6?
    ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    (a) A single approved supplier eliminates competitive bidding — the purchasing agent cannot use an alternate quote to drive down price, because the brand standard makes alternates non-compliant. The agent’s pricing leverage is essentially zero. (b) Tomás (the approved manufacturer’s rep) has more leverage, not less: the spec is locked to his line, competitive substitution is off the table, and the purchasing agent is obligated to buy from his manufacturer. Tomás’s commission is secure regardless of whether he negotiated harder than a competitor would have. (c) The fiduciary model becomes most important precisely when pricing leverage is constrained. If the agent cannot negotiate on price (because the supplier is mandated), the owner needs to be certain the agent’s income does not depend on volume or spread from that single supplier — otherwise the agent has no incentive to push back on pricing at all. A fiduciary agent on a fixed disclosed fee has the same incentive regardless of what the chair costs; a cost-plus agent earns more if the chair is more expensive.
