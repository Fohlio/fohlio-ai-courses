How Buildings Get Furnished

Chapter 2. Everyone who has to touch the project

Dana is on the phone before she has finished her coffee. The project — a 180-room lifestyle hotel in Austin — has been running for three weeks, and already there are four firms she has never met who need something from her by Friday. She puts down the phone and draws a quick chart on a notepad. By the time she is done there are eleven boxes connected by arrows, and three of them have the same client’s name written in different handwriting.

This is the moment every newcomer to the FF&E industry has. You come in thinking there are two parties — the person who buys the furniture and the person who sells it — and then someone explains that the buyer is actually six overlapping entities who each have a different claim on the budget, while the seller is a chain of four or five separate businesses who each earn their margin at a different step. The chart on Dana’s notepad is not unusual. It is what a single hotel project actually looks like.

This chapter is that chart, made legible. Take thirty seconds right now and try to name, from memory, every type of firm that you think is involved before a hotel room is furnished. Then read on.

Part 1. The demand side — four entities, one sign

The single most reliable source of confusion in a hospitality project is the name above the front door. Walk past a Marriott. The name on the sign implies one company. The reality is usually at least three, and sometimes four, distinct businesses with distinct rights over the budget:

Dana explains it to Priya the way she explains everything: by making her feel the pain first. “So you have Linda, the brand standards manager at the flag,” she says, pointing to a name on her notepad. “Her job is to make sure every Courtyard in the system looks and performs like a Courtyard. She does not own any of them.” She draws a second box. “The owner is a REIT — they put up the capital for this building, and they own the FF&E budget. They hired a management company to run the hotel day-to-day. And they signed a franchise agreement to fly the Courtyard flag, which means they have to satisfy Linda.” Priya stares at the three boxes. “So who does Dana work for?” “The owner. But we all have to make Linda happy.”

The four roles

Owner / developer / REIT: puts up the capital. Owns the real estate, owns the FF&E budget, and signs the final check. Typical examples: publicly-traded lodging REITs like Host Hotels & Resorts, private developers, or private-equity firms [1]. The owner is the primary client for the purchasing agent.

Brand / flag: Marriott alone operates more than 30 distinct brands across its Luxury, Premium, and Select tiers — Ritz-Carlton, St. Regis, W, Westin, Sheraton, Courtyard, and many more [1]. The brand mostly franchises or manages, not owns. Its leverage is the brand standard: a contractually enforceable specification of what every room must contain, down to the finishes, lighting, and approved furniture vendors. Meet it or lose the flag.

Management company: operates the hotel day-to-day under a management contract. Firms like Aimbridge Hospitality, Davidson Hospitality, and Renascent Hospitality run hotels on behalf of owners [2]. They care about operating costs and guest experience; they generally do not drive FF&E specification.

Franchisee: an owner who licenses the flag and is contractually bound to meet brand standards. The franchisee is often also the owner; it is a legal and commercial distinction rather than a separate company. The key point is that signing the franchise agreement creates a mandatory FF&E compliance obligation.

Part 2. The critical split: who specifies vs. who buys

In Lesson 1, we noted that Dana specified the sixty armchairs and a purchasing team bought them. That split is not a quirk of Dana’s studio — it is the foundational division of labor in the industry, and it has a very clear economic logic.

A single hotel opening can involve thousands of line items across dozens of vendor categories, with brand-standard compliance and a fixed opening date. No single designer can run the buying process while also running the design process. And no single buyer can make aesthetic and brand-compliance judgments that belong to the designer. The two roles require different skills, different relationships, and different legal accountability [2].

Interior design firms: they specify

The designer’s job is to choose every item — its model, finish, fabric, dimensions — and to produce the specification documents that instruct everyone else. Interior Design magazine’s 2025 Hospitality Giants rankings give a sense of the scale at the top of this profession:

  • HBA (Hirsch Bedner Associates) — $126.6 million in 2024 design fees; a staggering $8.17 billion in FF&E “value of work installed,” with 20+ offices worldwide [3].
  • Gensler ($44.2 million in hospitality fees; world’s largest architecture firm), Rockwell Group ($40 million), AvroKO ($20.7 million) [3].
  • Dozens of mid-size firms below them: ForrestPerkins, Wilson Associates, Yabu Pushelberg, Meyer Davis, The Gettys Group, and many regional studios like Dana’s.

Designers are paid for their creative and technical judgment. They do not take margin on the furniture they specify. Their compensation is fees — a fixed design fee or hourly — not a percentage of the purchase price.

FF&E purchasing agents: they buy

The purchasing agent — or procurement firm — turns the designer’s specification into budgets, competitive bids, purchase orders, and delivery. They manage the logistics, QC the goods at receiving, and hand over warranties and closeout documents at the end [2]. Their engagement typically begins before design is finished, precisely so that budgeting and value-engineering happen during the design phase rather than after orders are placed.

This is Marco’s world. In our story he works for a fiduciary purchasing firm in the mode of Benjamin West, one of the real firms that defines the category.

How purchasing agents get paid — the three models

This matters for anyone in sales or CS, because a prospect’s purchasing model shapes what they are buying from you and how they think about cost:

🤝
Fiduciary / disclosed fee
The industry standard in hospitality
The agent works for the owner, passes all trade discounts to the client, and is paid only its disclosed fee. The Benjamin West model. No hidden margin on product [2].
📊
Percentage / tiered fee
Common in mid-market
A management fee of roughly 10–20% of FF&E trade value, sometimes tiered (higher rate up to a threshold, lower above it). Casino RFQs literally ask agents to quote this rate [2].
💰
Principal / markup
More common in residential
The firm buys at trade (20–40% below retail) and resells at a markup — industry average around 35%. More common in residential design than hospitality procurement [2].
⚠️
Why it matters
Incentives shape behavior
A fiduciary agent has no incentive to pick pricier product. A markup agent does. Knowing which model a client uses explains a lot about how they evaluate vendor quotes.

Part 3. The supply side — who makes and sells the furniture

Manufacturers do not sell directly to hotel owners. In most cases, they sell through a chain of intermediaries, and understanding that chain is what separates the people who can hold a good GTM conversation from the people who just know product names.

Manufacturers: where the furniture actually comes from

The U.S. contract-furniture sector has clear leaders. Kimball Hospitality (based in Jasper, Indiana; part of Kimball International) supplies Marriott, Hilton, Hyatt, and IHG. Bernhardt Hospitality (North Carolina), Charter Furniture, and OFS Brands are the mid-tier anchors [4]. Below them sits an enormous overseas tier: Chinese manufacturers like CenSo Home and PAKEN, Turkish makers like MOBI and DRF, and Vietnamese factories like Tran Duc — chosen for cost and full customization at volume.

Custom millwork shops — the businesses that fabricate bespoke headboards, vanities, and reception desks — sit at the other extreme: highly specialized, domestic, and the longest-lead-time items on any project. Custom millwork commonly runs six to twelve months from order to delivery [5].

Manufacturer’s reps: the commission-only salespeople

Most contract furniture reaches designers and purchasing agents through a rep. Tomás, in our story, is a manufacturer’s rep — an independent salesperson who carries several non-competing product lines in a defined geographic territory, paid entirely on commission from the manufacturer [4].

Rep commissions in furniture run roughly 5–15% of revenue depending on the product and the relationship [4]. Their core job is not to close the transaction — it is to get products “specified into” a designer’s drawings, so that when the purchasing agent issues RFQs, their lines are on the list. Relationships with designers and purchasing firms are everything; the rep’s income depends on getting chosen before the order is placed.

This creates a specific dynamic: Tomás will always say yes to a lead time question, because saying “we can’t do it” loses the specification. He then has to check with the factory — which is a different conversation entirely. Marco knows this about reps, and builds it into his schedule assumptions.

Dealers, showrooms, and trade shows

Dealers are contracted resellers, especially dominant in the office and corporate furniture segment. Showrooms — permanent brand spaces in design centers like the Chicago Merchandise Mart or the Pacific Design Center — are where designers experience product in context. Trade shows are where the industry concentrates itself for a few days a year:

  • HD Expo (Hospitality Design) — Las Vegas; the flagship annual event for hospitality FF&E
  • NeoCon — Chicago Merchandise Mart; the largest commercial interiors event in North America
  • BDNY (Boutique Design New York) — the boutique and lifestyle hotel market

About 75% of hospitality furniture still sells through these direct, showroom, dealer, and consultant channels rather than online procurement [4]. The remaining 25% is growing — but this is emphatically still a relationship business.

Part 4. The logistics tail and the owner’s rep

Two more roles that often surprise outsiders: the people who actually put the furniture in the room, and the person whose entire job is to watch everyone else.

The 3PL / white-glove logistics firms

FF&E does not arrive at a hotel in one shipment. It comes from dozens of manufacturers on different schedules, via ocean freight, truck, and air, through customs if imported. Someone has to consolidate it in a regional warehouse, inspect it against the purchase orders within 24 hours, barcode-inventory it, and then deliver each piece to the right room on the right floor in the right order — because in a hotel you cannot move finished furniture through a crowded corridor after install.

That is Reggie’s job. In the real industry, firms like American Fargo (since 1949, an Atlas Van Lines partner), Corrigan Logistics (UniGroup/United Van Lines network), and Bailey’s Allied do this work [5]. They are not movers in the household sense. They are hospitality-installation specialists, and using a general mover instead is one of the most reliable ways to cause an expensive opening-day problem [5].

The receiving failure, revisited: in Lesson 1, Dana’s $40k mistake surfaced at receiving — when sixty chairs arrived in the wrong finish and nobody had caught it earlier. The receiving step is the last moment to catch a problem before it becomes a crisis. Skipping the inspection at the warehouse is cited as the single most common and most expensive logistics failure in the industry [5]. Reggie does not skip it.

The owner’s rep

Sam, the owner’s representative in our story, is the one actor in the chain who works for nobody except the owner. A GC builds what is drawn; the owner’s rep ensures what is being built makes operational and financial sense. On a large hotel project, the owner’s rep coordinates budgets, schedules, contracts, brand compliance, and the running mediation among owner, operator, designer, GC, and brand [2].

Sam is money-and-schedule realist by temperament and by job description. He is the one who calls Dana when the budget is over and asks what can be value-engineered. He is also the one who signs the final inspection.

The general contractor

The GC builds the shell and the fixed work. On most hotel projects, the GC and the FF&E team run on parallel tracks that converge at the end, when the building shell is finished enough for furniture installation to begin. The tension point: the GC’s schedule and the FF&E delivery schedule have to meet at the same place at the same time, and neither team controls the other’s timeline.

Part 6. Who pays whom — and who is free

One question clarifies more GTM conversations than almost any other: in this deal, who actually writes the checks?

The answer, compressed to its skeleton: the owner owns the budget and writes all the checks. Everyone else earns their compensation in one of four ways:

💳
Designer → owner
Design fees, paid by the owner. No margin on furniture.
💳
Purchasing agent → owner
Fiduciary fee, percentage of trade value, or markup — disclosed and paid by the owner. Not by the manufacturer.
💳
Manufacturer’s rep → manufacturer
Commission from the manufacturer, not from the designer or buyer. The rep is free to the buying side.
💳
Brand → franchisee
The brand earns royalty fees from the franchisee/owner. It does not pay for the furniture; it mandates what furniture must be bought.

The 3PL logistics firm is paid by the owner (often billed through the purchasing agent). The GC is paid by the owner. The owner’s rep is paid by the owner. When you trace the money, all roads go to the owner.

A useful frame for sales conversations: the owner holds the budget; the designer holds the specification; the purchasing agent holds the transaction. When any one of these three says “we can’t do it,” that is the veto that matters. The brand has a veto on what is specified; the owner has a veto on what is paid for. Understanding which veto is live in a given prospect situation is the difference between a productive discovery call and a stuck deal.
