From a Moodboard to a Spec

Chapter 4. A Pinterest image has no model number, no finish, no lead time — and a hotel opens in eight months

The client’s email arrives at 9:03 a.m. on a Monday. It contains forty-seven images. Lobby chairs in burnished cognac leather. Bedside lamps with hand-thrown ceramic bases. A headboard draped in something that looks like linen — or possibly a woven wool blend. No manufacturer names. No model numbers. Not a single dimension. Just the caption: “This is the vibe we’re going for.”

Dana reads it twice. Priya, leaning over her shoulder, says: “Great! So now we just… find those things?”

Dana pulls up a fresh spreadsheet. “We find things that become those things,” she says. “That’s a completely different job.”

This chapter is about that job: how a mood, an image, a brand book, or a design concept becomes a real, buyable, orderable, trackable specification line. It is one of the most misunderstood translations in the entire FF&E business — and the moment it goes wrong is almost always right here, before a single item has been selected.

  • The difference between a spec sheet, a cut sheet, and a tear sheet — and which one actually commits the designer to something

  • What a spec book contains and who it is for
  • Why brand standards from a flag like Marriott or Hilton enter during early design — not at the end — and what happens if they enter late
  • Precisely why a Pinterest picture is not yet a spec line, and what five things it is missing

Part 1. Why a picture isn’t a spec

A moodboard is a collection of feelings. An FF&E specification is a collection of commitments. The gap between them is not style — it is data.

Priya is looking at the client’s forty-seven images. “That cognac leather chair — can’t we just send this photo to vendors and ask who makes something like it?”

Dana can, and sometimes does, exactly that. But “something like it” is not a spec. For a picture to become a line in an FF&E schedule — the master document that tracks the whole project — it needs five things the image does not contain [1]:

🏷
Manufacturer & model
Who makes it, what exact product line, what model number. Without this there is nothing to order.
🎨
Finish & material
The exact fabric grade, leather grade, wood stain, metal finish — specified down to the dye lot if custom. “Cognac-ish” is not a finish.
📏
Dimensions & ADA clearances
The item has to fit — in the space, within fire-egress clearances, and in compliance with accessibility requirements.
💰
Price & trade source
Which vendor, at what trade price. Not the retail tag in the image caption — the price the firm actually pays, from the source they’ve approved.
⏰
Lead time
How long from order to delivery. A beautiful chair that ships in 26 weeks is a beautiful problem if the hotel opens in 18.

Until those five things are pinned down, the image is inspiration. The moment all five are attached to a product record that a purchasing agent can act on, it becomes a specification line. The entire journey from moodboard to spec is the process of filling in those blanks — phase by phase, with sign-offs at each gate.

Part 2. Three phases, three levels of commitment

The AIA — the American Institute of Architects — publishes dedicated contracts for FF&E work (B152, B252) that follow the same phase structure as architecture. The industry has used this vocabulary for decades, and Dana uses it on every engagement [2]. Understanding what happens in each phase is what lets you have an intelligent conversation with any designer or purchasing agent.

Programming: scope, not solutions

Programming is what the industry sometimes calls “problem-seeking.” No products are selected here. The team establishes scope: how many rooms, what mix (kings, queens, suites), how many F&B seats, what the budget per key looks like, and — critically — whether the property is flagged under a brand standard like Marriott or IHG. That last question turns out to matter enormously [3].

The output of Programming is a preliminary FF&E budget carrying a large contingency — often 25–30% — because almost everything is still unknown. It is a planning number, not a commitment. The word “contingency” is doing heavy lifting.

Schematic Design (SD): furniture layouts, no products yet

The designer produces furniture layouts using generic placeholder blocks. Not a specific chair — a rectangle labeled “lounge seating” at roughly the right scale. The point is to test ADA clearances, traffic flow, and adjacencies against the floor plan. Clients react to scale and arrangement. Specific SKUs are not yet relevant [3].

This is also when designers attend trade shows. HD Expo, BDNY (Boutique Design New York), ICFF — these are where the cognac leather chairs and hand-thrown lamps in the client’s email actually come from. Designers walk the floor, pick up business cards, collect product samples, and build a mental catalog of what is possible before any of it becomes a commitment.

Sign-off gate: the client approves one concept direction. Everything that follows develops that direction, not all of them.

Design Development (DD): the phase where specs actually get built

This is the critical one. Specific product families get selected. The designer begins producing cut sheets — documents with exact dimensions, finishes, and COM (Customer’s Own Material) specifications when the designer is supplying a custom fabric. Materials are reviewed in person. Lighting is studied for proportion and output. The contingency drops to about 15–20% [3].

This is the phase where the FF&E specification is effectively built — where that cognac leather chair stops being a mood and becomes “Vendor: Bernhardt Contract, Model: Sutton Lounge Chair, Finish: Cognac Grade 3 leather, Dimensions: 30”W × 32”D × 34”H, Lead time: 14 weeks.”

Priya looks at the cut sheet Dana is building for the lounge chair. “This took you three hours for one chair,” she says. Dana doesn’t look up. “There are 180 chairs in this hotel. And that’s just the chairs.”

The lobby alone has six room-types of seating, each with its own spec. When the spec is wrong, you find out at the dock, with an opening date that doesn’t move. Dana has a sticky note on her monitor that says “The spec was right the day I sent it.” Priya is beginning to understand why.

Part 3. The documents — spec sheet, cut sheet, tear sheet

Three terms that sound interchangeable and are not. The industry uses them loosely in conversation, but they describe three different levels of depth, and confusing them causes real problems [1].

Tear sheet = quick look; cut sheet = dimensional detail; spec sheet = the full record the purchasing agent acts on.

In practice, many people use “cut sheet” and “spec sheet” interchangeably, and “tear sheet” for the lightest version. What matters is the distinction in depth: a tear sheet is enough for a presentation; a spec sheet is what a purchasing agent needs to write a purchase order. A cut sheet with full COM specs and shop drawings is what a custom manufacturer needs to build the thing.

📋
Priya, handing Dana a folder: “I pulled all the tear sheets for the lobby seating.”
Dana: “Great. Now tell Marco what he’s supposed to order from them.”
Priya (staring at the folder): “…the vibes?”
A tear sheet is the beginning of a conversation, not the end of one. The spec sheet is what ends it.

Part 4. The FF&E schedule vs. the specification — two different jobs

This is the distinction that trips up almost everyone outside the industry, and it matters because confusing the two creates real project failures.

The specification (the spec sheet) describes one product: what it is, who makes it, what it looks like, how it’s configured. It is a product record. It answers the question “what are we buying?”

The FF&E schedule tracks the whole project: every item, across every room type, with vendor, quantity, unit cost, total cost, lead time, order status, and delivery date. A residential project has 50–200 lines. A full hospitality project has 500 to 5,000+ [1]. It answers “where does every item stand right now?”

The spec describes what you’re buying. The schedule tracks whether you’ve bought it, when it will arrive, and what it will cost.

Think of it this way: every row in the FF&E schedule points at a spec. The spec is the source of truth for that product. The schedule is the source of truth for the project’s overall status. When Dana’s sixty armchairs arrived in the wrong finish — the $40k mistake from Lesson 1 — the spec was correct and the schedule showed “ordered.” What neither document captured was that the finish had gone stale in the gap. That is the gap the industry is constantly trying to close.

The finish schedule: a different document entirely

Alongside the FF&E schedule sits a separate document: the finish schedule. This lists the hard finishes by room — flooring type, wall covering, paint color, tile — and is distinct from the furniture schedule [4]. Both schedules are produced during Design Development, both are part of the handover package to the owner, and they live in different columns for a reason: finishes touch the Finishes and Architectural segments (the things that don’t fall out when you shake the building), while the FF&E schedule owns the movable furnishings.

Part 5. The spec book — and who it is for

At the end of Design Development and into the Construction Documents phase, the designer compiles every spec sheet into a single comprehensive document: the spec book. Its purpose is coordination — to align the owner, the brand (on flagged properties), the purchasing agent, and the manufacturers on exactly what has been specified [1].

A spec book for a 200-room hotel might be 400–800 pages. It covers every room type. Every item is keyed to its room code (so the purchasing agent knows that “GR-03” is the guestroom lounge chair, not the lobby lounge chair). Every fabric is swatched. Every fire-rating certificate is listed.

Who reads the spec book? The purchasing agent (Marco) uses it to cross-reference the FF&E schedule and catch anything missing. The brand standards manager (Linda) uses it to verify compliance with the flag’s approved product list. The manufacturer uses it to confirm they are building exactly what was specified. The owner’s rep uses it at handover to confirm what was delivered versus what was specified.

The spec book is the closest thing the industry has to a contract for product — it is what people reach for when there is a dispute about what was agreed. “I never approved that finish” is a sentence that can cost hundreds of thousands of dollars; the spec book and its sign-off trail are the evidence that settles it.

✋ Explain before continuing
Without looking back: what is the difference between the FF&E schedule and the finish schedule? Name one thing that appears in each. Why do hotels track them as separate documents rather than one master list?
Think about which segment each document lives in. One tracks the movable; the other tracks the applied surfaces. They have different owners at different points in the project.

Part 6. Brand standards enter in Schematic Design — not at the end

If the project is flagged — operating under a brand agreement with Marriott, Hilton, IHG, Hyatt, or another flag — the brand’s standards do not arrive as a checklist at the end of Design Development. They arrive in Programming and shape every decision from the first furniture layout onward [5].

This is the part that surprises people outside the industry. A brand standard is not a style guide. It is a contractually enforceable document that specifies approved product categories, approved finish ranges, required fire ratings, minimum furniture dimensions, and sometimes specific approved manufacturers. A hotel operating under a Marriott flag cannot simply decide to use a different headboard because it looks better. The headboard must conform to the brand’s current approved specifications, or a formal exception process must be triggered — which takes time and sometimes gets denied.

Dana is building the initial SD furniture plan when Priya asks why she is cross-referencing a 200-page PDF of brand requirements at every step. “Because Linda,” Dana says. Linda is the brand standards manager at the flag, and she has the authority to reject any finish that isn’t on the approved list, regardless of how beautiful it is. Dana learned this early: “You don’t design for the flag and then check the standards. You design within the standards from day one. Otherwise you design twice.”

We’ll meet Linda in full in Lesson 6. For now, the key fact is that brand standards are a constraint that enters the room in Phase 1 — not Phase 4.

For unflagged properties — independent boutique hotels, Grace’s inn from Lesson 2 — this constraint doesn’t apply, and design freedom is much broader. The trade-off is that there is no pre-approved product list, no brand library to start from, no pre-negotiated vendor pricing. Every selection has to be built from scratch. Freedom has a cost in time, and Grace is doing it all herself, with no purchasing agent backstop.

Part 7. Putting it together: the chain from image to order

Let’s trace Dana’s cognac leather chair the full way — from the client’s email to a line on the FF&E schedule that Marco can act on. This is the chain in practice, and Lesson 3’s long view of the full project timeline shows where it sits: the spec gets built in parallel with early construction, because the moment the spec is final is also (roughly) the moment the purchasing agent needs to start placing orders to hit the opening date [2].

1 Client shares moodboard image
A picture of a cognac leather lounge chair, probably from a design blog or a competitor hotel. No model, no finish grade, no dimensions. Starting point only.
2 Designer researches candidates (SD)
Dana pulls tear sheets from trade show contacts, vendor catalogs, and prior projects. Three or four candidate chairs emerge. All are checked against brand standard color and material approval lists.
3 Material samples reviewed in person (DD)
Physical leather swatches arrive from two manufacturers. The team compares grade, texture, durability rating. One is rejected on durability (a hotel chair gets used by hundreds of guests per year). COM fabric option from a separate textile house is considered.
4 Cut sheet built, dimensions verified (DD)
Dana drafts the cut sheet: manufacturer, model, exact finish spec (Grade 3 leather, Cognac colorway), dimensions confirmed against the floor plan ADA clearances, lead time confirmed with the manufacturer’s rep as 14 weeks.
5 Spec sheet completed, added to spec book
The full spec sheet is built: model, finish, source, price (trade), lead time, fire-rating certificate, warranty. It goes into the spec book under the guestroom lounge chair code (GR-06).
6 Line added to FF&E schedule
GR-06 lands in the FF&E schedule: vendor, qty (180 chairs), unit cost, total, lead time, status “Specified / Pending PO.” Marco can now act on it. The image from the client’s email is, finally, a spec line.

Six steps for one chair — times 500 to 5,000 items: this is why the FF&E process takes 12–24 months and why a purchasing firm like Benjamin West or Beyer Brown earns its fee. The moodboard is the beginning of the work, not a summary of it.

    ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    A strong answer identifies that moodboard images typically show: the approximate shape/style (partial) and sometimes the material/color (partial) — but almost never the manufacturer/model, the exact finish grade, confirmed ADA dimensions, trade price, or lead time. The purchasing agent’s problem is that they cannot write a purchase order without those fields: the vendor needs a model number, the site needs to know the item fits the space legally, and the schedule needs a lead time to determine when the order must be placed against the opening date.
  2. Distinguish the documents. Without looking back at the lesson, write one sentence defining each of these: (a) tear sheet, (b) cut sheet, (c) spec sheet, (d) FF&E schedule, (e) finish schedule, (f) spec book. Then answer: if you were Marco the purchasing agent and you had just received the spec book for a 150-room hotel, what would you use it for in the next two weeks?
    ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    (a) Tear sheet: a quick one-page product reference, often clipped from a vendor catalog, for presentation or vendor conversations. Not a commitment. (b) Cut sheet: detailed document with exact dimensions, materials, and often installation/COM specs; used as the basis for ordering and for custom fabrication. (c) Spec sheet: the comprehensive record of one product — model, finish, price, lead time, fire rating, warranties — that a purchasing agent acts on. (d) FF&E schedule: the project-wide master tracking document for all items, covering vendor, quantity, cost, lead time, and status. (e) Finish schedule: a parallel document listing hard finishes by room — flooring, paint, wallcovering, tile — distinct from the furniture schedule. (f) Spec book: all spec sheets compiled into one aligned document for owner, brand, purchasing agent, and manufacturers. Marco’s immediate task: cross-reference every item in the spec book against the FF&E schedule to catch anything missing or inconsistent, then begin issuing RFQs to shortlisted vendors for the high-lead-time items.
