Vendors, Manufacturers, Reps and Sources

Chapter 9. Marco calls Tomás, and Tomás calls Greta

Tomás Vega answers on the second ring. He is driving between a showroom in San Francisco and a jobsite meeting in Oakland, and he already knows why Marco is calling. There are forty-eight lounge chairs on a spec sheet, upholstered in a tight-weave fabric that Tomás represents for three different manufacturers. He carries the lines, he knows the factories, and he has sold into this kind of project a hundred times. He is not the manufacturer. He does not make the chairs. The manufacturer does not pay Marco for Tomás’s time. The manufacturer pays Tomás, after the chairs ship, a commission out of the sale price.

“Let me check with the factory,” Tomás says. He says this in almost every call. He means it.

Greta, at the factory, picks up a few hours later. She is a production coordinator at a contract furniture manufacturer. She does not sell anything. She makes things — or more precisely, she manages the process of making things, in quantities that meet the minimums her factory requires to actually run a production line. “We don’t do under thirty units,” she says, before Tomás has finished the sentence about the forty-eight chairs. Forty-eight clears the threshold. They talk about lead time.

This three-person chain — Marco the procurement lead, Tomás the rep, Greta the factory — is how most contract furniture in the United States actually moves from concept to chair. This lesson names every layer of it.

Part 1. Who actually makes the chairs

The “supply side” in FF&E is not one thing. It is a layered ecosystem of manufacturers, intermediaries, and channel partners that evolved over decades to get contract-grade furniture from factories to projects at the scale and customization level that branded hospitality requires. Understanding who does what is one of the most practically useful things a GTM team can know.

US contract and hospitality furniture manufacturers

The contract furniture industry has a defined group of domestic manufacturers that the hospitality market knows by name. Their products appear in spec sheets across Marriott, Hilton, Hyatt, and IHG properties because they have earned brand approval, passed fire-rating certification, and demonstrated the ability to produce consistent quality at hotel volume [1].

🏭
Kimball Hospitality
Jasper, Indiana. Part of Kimball International. Supplies Marriott, Hilton, Hyatt, IHG. One of the largest US contract hospitality furniture makers.
🏭
Bernhardt Hospitality
North Carolina. Bernhardt traces to 1889. Full hospitality casegoods and seating; known for craftsmanship on upper-upscale and luxury projects.
🏭
OFS Brands / KI
OFS (Indiana) and KI (Green Bay, WI) are strong in the corporate / contract side but appear in hospitality public-area and meeting-room specs.
🏭
Global Furniture Group
Canada-based, ~$1B in revenue. Contract and hospitality across healthcare, senior living, and hotel verticals.

These are domestic manufacturers in the sense that their main operations are in North America. Their lead times — typically 8–16 weeks for standard-finish items — are more predictable than overseas sourcing, and their goods face no import duties. In the current tariff environment (see Lesson 8), that last point has become a genuine competitive advantage rather than just a marketing line [2].

Overseas manufacturers: China, Vietnam, Turkey, Indonesia

A large share of hospitality furniture — particularly the volume casegoods (dressers, desks, nightstands) that fill hundreds of identical guestrooms — is manufactured overseas, primarily in China and Vietnam, with Turkey and Indonesia as significant secondary sources. The cost advantage is real: overseas production at volume can be 20–40% cheaper than equivalent domestic production before tariffs. After applying the full tariff stack from Lesson 8, that advantage narrows significantly, and the calculation changes again when freight rates spike [2].

Marco’s current decision rule on sourcing country: “If I can get a domestic manufacturer to produce within 15% of the overseas landed cost, I take the domestic source. The certainty of delivery is worth the premium every time I have a hard opening date.”

Custom millwork: the longest lead, highest risk

One category sits apart from all others: custom millwork — the built-in and bespoke joinery that a catalog cannot supply. Reception desks, custom headboard panels, vanity casework, bar fronts, decorative shelving built to a specific dimension. Millwork is made by specialized shops, not mass-production factories. It requires detailed shop drawings, sample approvals, and often multiple rounds of mock-ups.

Lead time: 6–12 months for complex custom millwork. That is not a misprint. A hotel lobby reception desk that requires a custom stone inlay, a specific wood veneer, and a proprietary finish can take as long to produce as a mid-size factory needs to build a standard guestroom package. Procurement teams that treat millwork the same as standard furniture — starting the process when other items start — cause the single most common cause of delayed hotel openings that Marco knows [1].

Part 2. The rep model — the invisible layer in every spec

Most people who buy furniture professionally have never spoken directly with a manufacturer. They speak with a manufacturer’s rep, and they may not even know that’s what they are doing. The rep model is the structural backbone of how contract furniture moves from maker to specifier.

How it works

A manufacturer’s rep is an independent salesperson who represents multiple manufacturers’ product lines within a defined geographic territory. They are not employees of any manufacturer. They carry their lines under contract, earn commission on sales, and are free to carry other lines so long as those lines do not compete directly with one another [3].

Commission on contract furniture typically runs around 5% of revenue for contract lines; in design contexts where the rep is actively managing A&D firm relationships and getting products specified, the effective rate can run higher — commonly cited as 7–15% from the manufacturer [3]. The commission is paid by the manufacturer, not by the buyer. From the buyer’s perspective, the rep is free.

The rep’s commission is embedded in the manufacturer’s net pricing structure. It never appears on the buyer’s invoice — but it is always there.

What the rep actually does

Tomás’s job is not closing transactions. It is getting his lines’ products into designers’ specifications before the designer starts writing the spec. That process — called getting a product “specified” — requires years of relationship-building with A&D firms: leaving samples, hosting lunch-and-learns, walking into showrooms, following up when a designer is starting a project that might use a product from one of his lines.

When Tomás gets a product specified into Dana’s drawings, his manufacturer’s line has a structural advantage in the RFQ that follows. Dana already knows the product, has approved samples, and is unlikely to substitute unless the price is dramatically wrong or the lead time is impossible. That is the commercial logic behind the rep model: manufacturers outsource distribution and specification-development to independent reps who already have the relationships, rather than running their own national sales forces [3].

💼
Tomás carries six lines. He represents a high-end seating manufacturer, two casegoods factories, a lighting house, a decorative accessory supplier, and a company that makes the little things nobody specifies until they are missing from the room. He does not find this unusual.

A designer once asked him: “Isn’t it a conflict of interest to represent multiple manufacturers?” Tomás explained that the lines are non-competing by design — you would never carry two seating factories with identical products. The whole system depends on that structure: one rep, complementary lines, one relationship for the designer to manage instead of six.

Part 3. Dealers, showrooms, distributors, and where they fit

The rep is not the only channel between manufacturer and buyer. Depending on the product category and the scale of the project, goods may also move through dealers, showrooms, or distributors — each with a different role and a different cost structure.

📄
Dealer
Contracted reseller
Especially common in the office/contract furniture world. A dealer has a formal reseller agreement with a manufacturer (e.g. Steelcase, Herman Miller), carries their products, handles customer service and local installation. The manufacturer sells through them rather than direct. Dealers often have their own design services.
🏛
Showroom
Where designers see product
Physical spaces — often in design-district buildings — where designers can touch, sit in, and view products in a curated environment. Major design centers include New York (D&D Building), Chicago, Los Angeles, and Atlanta. Showrooms may carry multiple brands under one roof or be brand-exclusive.
📦
Distributor
Stock for faster fulfillment
Carries inventory, enabling faster delivery than a make-to-order manufacturer. More common for accessories, lighting, and standard (non-custom) items where speed matters more than customization. A distributor buys from the manufacturer and resells at a markup.
🎪
Trade shows
Where relationships are made
The three dominant shows in hospitality FF&E: HD Expo (Las Vegas, hospitality design), NeoCon (Chicago, contract / commercial), BDNY (New York, boutique design). Designers, reps, and manufacturers meet, new lines launch, sample rooms are previewed. Specifiers discover new lines here before they show up on any spec sheet.

About 75% of hospitality furniture still moves through offline channels — direct from manufacturer (via rep), through dealer, or through showroom relationship — rather than any digital procurement platform [1]. The rep model is old technology in the best sense: it has survived because the relationships and the specification process require human presence and trust that a catalog page cannot replicate.

Part 4. The sourcing decision: domestic vs. import, MOQ, and lead time

When Marco receives Dana’s spec for the forty-eight lounge chairs, the sourcing decision is not simply “cheapest price.” It is a structured calculation across four variables that all interact.

Variable What it means in practice How it constrains the decision
Unit cost The product price before landed-cost layers (see L8) Overseas often wins on unit cost; domestic wins on landed cost after tariffs
MOQ Minimum order quantity per SKU; typically 20–200 for hotel furniture [4] An order that clears MOQ cleanly has pricing and timeline certainty; one that doesn’t requires negotiation or a split-run premium
Lead time Weeks from approved PO to goods at the warehouse; custom: 30–90 days production + 20–40 days transit for sea freight [4] A hard hotel opening date sets a back-calculated PO deadline; any lead time that pushes past it forces a schedule decision
Tariff exposure Current import duty stack for the specific HTS code and origin country (see L8 for current ranges) China-origin furniture carries ~30–35%+ stacked duty as of mid-2026; Vietnam ~20%; domestic = no duty. Re-verify before any PO. [2]
✋ Apply the framework before reading on
Greta’s Vietnam factory quotes 48 lounge chairs at $290/unit, MOQ 30, lead time 14 weeks production + 5 weeks ocean freight. A US manufacturer offers the same chair at $420/unit, MOQ 12, lead time 10 weeks, no import duty. The hotel opens in 22 weeks from today’s PO. Which source is cheaper landed? Which has more schedule risk? Does Marco’s rule from Part 1 change the answer?
Vietnam landed: add ~35% for tariff, freight, brokerage, warehousing, install from L8. Total lead time for Vietnam: 14 + 5 = 19 weeks. Buffer before 22-week deadline: how many weeks? What does a 3-week buffer mean in a hospitality context?

Marco’s rule in practice: when domestic landed cost is within 15% of the overseas landed cost, take domestic. The buffer in schedule certainty, the elimination of tariff risk, and the ability to make a last-minute finish change without a transatlantic phone call are worth the premium on any project with a hard opening date and a brand standard that enforces consistency [2].

Tariff figures are dated and volatile. The rates cited above reflect public information as of mid-2026 and can change by presidential proclamation. Before making any sourcing decision, re-verify at hts.usitc.gov and ustr.gov.

Part 5. Vendor scorecards — the intelligence that compounds

Marco has been working with Tomás for six years. He has a mental model of exactly what Tomás’s factories deliver: which lines run on schedule, which have a tendency to substitute a “close enough” finish when the specified one runs out, which factories have expanded capacity in the last year and which are running at their ceiling. That mental model is vendor intelligence — and the risk is that it lives entirely in Marco’s head. When Marco is on vacation, it is nowhere.

A vendor scorecard makes that intelligence structured and sharable. The fields that matter are not complicated, but they require discipline to populate after every project closes [1]:

💰
PO volume
Total dollar amount placed with this vendor across projects. Volume determines negotiating leverage and whether you qualify for volume incentive rebates.
📋
Spec count
How many times this vendor’s products have appeared in a specification. High spec count means the rep is doing their job and the product has designer traction.
⏱
Lead-time accuracy
Did goods arrive when promised? Over time, a vendor with a consistent 2-week overrun should be booked 2 weeks earlier. That data is only useful if it is recorded.
⭐
Quality rating
How often did goods arrive as specified, undamaged, correct finish? A vendor with a beautiful catalog and a chronic damage rate is a budget risk on every project.
📄
Payment terms / credit limit
The terms this vendor has extended and what credit limit is available. Purchasing firms managing cash flow across multiple projects track this carefully.

The insight from Lesson 2: the value chain in FF&E runs from owner through designer through purchasing agent to the supply side and back. Every PO that closes is a data point about that supply side. Firms that capture that data — lead-time accuracy, quality ratings, actual vs. quoted price — arrive at every future project knowing things that competitors have to discover expensively from scratch.

Part 6. GPOs — what they are and where they don’t work

In Lesson 6 we saw how brand standards work at the property level: a flag like Marriott defines what a room must contain and enforces it through PIPs. There is a separate mechanism for aggregating operational spend across many properties: the Group Purchasing Organization, or GPO.

The largest GPO in North American hospitality is Avendra, now operating as Avendra International under Aramark, which acquired it for approximately $1.3 billion in 2017 [5]. Avendra aggregates purchasing power across thousands of hotel properties to negotiate volume discounts on food, beverages, cleaning supplies, linens, and other OS&E categories. At its peak, member spend ran in the billions of dollars annually.

GPOs work exceptionally well for standardized, recurring OS&E spend: the kind of item that every property in a chain uses in the same quantity, at predictable intervals, without customization. A hotel chain using a GPO for its laundry detergent or its flat-screen televisions is getting real leverage on real volume.

GPOs work poorly — or not at all — for project FF&E: the bespoke, one-time capital purchase that varies by property, requires brand standard compliance on specific finish details, and is not standardized across a portfolio. A boutique hotel’s custom lounge chair with a proprietary fabric is not a GPO-compatible purchase. Neither is a luxury hotel’s hand-knotted lobby rug. The sourcing has to happen at the project level, with the design intent preserved, through the procurement firm model described in Lesson 2 [1].

✅
GPO works for…
Standardized OS&E
Food and beverage, cleaning supplies, linens, towels, glassware, tech commodities. Same SKU across many properties. Volume = leverage.
❌
GPO does NOT work for…
Project FF&E
Custom casegoods, branded fabric, bespoke millwork, proprietary finishes. Different every project. Requires design intent + brand standard compliance on each line item.

Part 7. Vendors as structured data — a thin Fohlio aside

Marco keeps a contact book. It started as a spreadsheet — company name, office location, contact person, what they sell. Over years it has grown into something closer to a vendor intelligence database: each entry carries the name of a firm and the specific office Marco deals with (the New York showroom of a manufacturer is a different commercial relationship than the Chicago rep office, even though both have the same company name on the door).

In the platform Fohlio’s team uses internally, vendors live as sources in a contact book structured as company plus office. A single chair in a spec can carry multiple source entries — different vendors who can supply it — so the procurement team can compare options before committing. Each source record can carry the fields that make it vendor intelligence rather than just a directory: the SKU for that vendor’s version of the product, the unit price and any discount tier, the lead time on record, the MOQ, the payment terms the vendor extends, and a preferred-source flag when one option is consistently the right call.

Over multiple projects, those records accumulate into exactly the scorecard described in Part 5 — the lead-time accuracy, the quality history, the volume-over-time that determines rebate eligibility. The difference between a contact book and a vendor intelligence system is not the data fields themselves: it is whether the data is updated after every project closes, and whether it is available to someone other than the person who collected it.

Chapter summary

The supply side is not a monolith. It is a layered ecosystem: manufacturers who make, reps who specify, dealers and distributors who move product through channels, showrooms and trade shows where the relationships are built. Understanding who is who in that ecosystem is the difference between a procurement team that manages the supply side and one that is managed by it.
