Chapter 2. How “I want it cozy” becomes the first line of the spec

The moodboard is forty-seven images pinned to a corkboard in the conference room, and Priya is staring at it like it owes her something. Warm amber lighting. Linen textures. A velvet armchair photographed at golden hour. And dead center — one image clipped from Pinterest with a stock-photo watermark stretched across the seat.

The client’s creative brief says: “Cozy, warm, residential. Mountain lodge meets Scandinavian minimalism.”

Dana walks in, sets her coffee down, and looks at the board for four seconds. “Okay,” she says. “Which one is the chair?”

Priya points at the watermarked image.

“Find me the manufacturer, the model name, the finish options, and the lead time. Then it’s a line item. Until then, it’s a feeling.”

That gap — between a feeling and a line item — is Phase 1. It’s where design intent lives. And it’s the most porous phase in the entire pipeline, because nothing here is data yet. Every project starts exactly here, and the $40k mistake from Lesson 1 was already taking shape in this room — months before the chairs arrived at the warehouse.

Today — L2: Phase 1 (design intent). Where the very first line item comes from — and why a moodboard image isn’t data.

Part 1. What “design intent” actually means

Interior designers use “design intent” the way engineers use “requirements”: loosely, with the private understanding that the real thing is always messier than the word implies.

Design intent is the answer to: what should this space feel like, and what should go in it? It predates products. It predates prices. Sometimes it predates measurements. A client sits across from a designer and says “I want it to feel like the lobby of that hotel in Copenhagen” — and that sentence is design intent. Nothing in it is purchasable.

For the specifier, Phase 1 has one job: extract enough signal from the client to go shopping. Not vague shopping. Specific: objects from specific suppliers at specific prices. The distance between “feels like Copenhagen” and “Herman Miller Aeron, task chair, graphite finish, qty 24, lead time 8 weeks” is the entire substance of Phase 1 [1].

For the engineer: Phase 1 produces no structured data that enters the system directly. Moodboards are not imported. Pinterest pins are not rows. What enters the spec is always a human decision made by Dana — one product at a time. Phase 1 is the upstream of everything we model, but it lives entirely outside the database. This matters for product scope.

That’s the awkward truth about Phase 1: it matters enormously, and it’s almost entirely unstructured. Engineers instinctively want to pipeline it. “Can’t we just import the Pinterest board?” Dana’s answer is some version of: “Import a photograph of a watermarked armchair from a discontinued Finnish brand and tell me which SKU you want to order.”

Part 2. Three clients, three very different inputs

Phase 1 looks completely different depending on who walks through the door. Watching all three clients arrive here is the fastest way to understand what the domain is actually managing.

Marriott — Linda, brand manager. Linda doesn’t arrive with a moodboard. She arrives with a PDF. Sixty-two pages. The Marriott Select Brand Standard for guest room FF&E, revision 4.1. Approved manufacturers. Approved finish codes. Approved quantities per room type. A list of products that are not substitutable under any circumstances. The “intent” is already formalized to near-product level. Dana’s Phase 1 here isn’t about identifying products — it’s about mapping the brand standard onto this property’s specific room count and configuration.

In the system: Marriott’s brand standard becomes a BrandStandard entity in the workspace — a catalog of pre-approved products. Dana’s job in Phase 1 is to pull from it, not discover new products. Lesson 5 covers how that works mechanically.

Grace — Cedar & Oak, boutique inn. Grace shows up with her phone. She opens Pinterest. She has 340 pins saved over four months, organized into boards named “vibes,” “maybe dining room,” and “YES THIS.” No brand standard. No preferred vendor list. No procurement department. A vision and a budget she described as “flexible, but not, like, flexible.” Phase 1 for Grace is the full funnel: identification, verification, and painful price reality checks, all performed by Dana with minimal scaffolding from the client.

Most SMB projects follow this pattern. The specifier acts as researcher, translator, and filter. The system must support a high volume of exploratory items that may never make the final spec.

The Church of Jesus Christ — Brother Andersen. Brother Andersen emails a ZIP. Inside: a standard album for meetinghouse furnishings, last revised two years ago. Approved products with part numbers. Preferred vendors. Quantities per room type. Cost targets per unit. The output of a years-long value-engineering exercise. Phase 1 for the Church is almost a formality — but not quite. The standard must be verified against current supplier availability. Products go out of stock. Vendors change pricing. The album may be two years old; the market is not. Nobody in that ZIP file knows what got discontinued last quarter.

The Church pattern is the extreme replication case: design intent was resolved once, long ago. Phase 1 here is “validate that the intent is still purchasable” rather than “discover intent.” The $40k mistake Dana carries from Lesson 1 is exactly this failure — intent was valid when specified, then went stale. Lesson 8 closes that loop.

Part 3. The moodboard — what’s in it and what’s not

A moodboard is a collection of reference images assembled to communicate aesthetic direction. That’s the kind definition. The less kind definition: a collage of things the client saw on Instagram and wants, without knowing whether they exist at a price they can afford.

What a moodboard contains:

🖼
Mood imagery
Photos capturing a feeling: light quality, texture, color palette. Rarely actual product shots.
📍
Reference products
Sometimes real products — but the photo is rarely the orderable version. Color shifts. Finish names differ by region. The model may have been discontinued.
🎨
Material samples
Fabric swatches, tile chips, paint cards. These can translate to spec finishes — but the finish name still requires verification against the supplier’s current catalog.
❌
Not present
Model numbers. Lead times. Prices. Quantities. Supplier contacts. Any of the things that make a line item purchasable.
📌
A reconstructed conversation, documented across many projects:

Grace: “I found the perfect chair — I can send you the Pinterest pin.”
Dana: “Manufacturer? Model? Finish? Price? Supplier?”
Grace: “It says ‘cozy amber oak armchair vintage vibes’…”
Dana: “Those are tags. Customs doesn’t accept tags.”

Pinterest has no “SKU” field. That’s why a pin is the start of a conversation, not a row in the spec. Every specifier learns this in the first week. Some clients never do.

Part 4. Pinterest and Revit — two different inputs

Fohlio integrates with two tools that live in Phase 1. They are not interchangeable. The difference tells you something important about where design intent comes from in different contexts.

Pinterest: consumer-grade signal

Pinterest is where most residential and small commercial clients build their reference boards. Images are optimized for aesthetic appeal, not product traceability. Most pins have no manufacturer attribution. Those that do link to retailer pages that may no longer carry the item.

Fohlio’s Pinterest integration pulls images from a client’s board into the project’s inspiration area. This is not importing products — it’s importing references. The specifier annotates each image, links it to a product she subsequently finds, and traces the client’s original intent once a real line item is in place. The image stays as provenance. The product is what matters [2].

Revit: architectural-grade signal

Revit is Autodesk’s BIM tool, used by architects and large commercial design firms. A Revit model contains structured data: rooms with dimensions, material specifications, sometimes furniture families tagged with manufacturer names. Intent that’s already been partly formalized.

Fohlio’s Revit integration imports room data and preliminary product specs into a project’s area structure. A “guest room, 28 m²” in Revit becomes an Area in Fohlio; a Revit furniture family tagged “lounge chair, Knoll” can seed a spec item. The specifier still verifies and confirms — but the starting point is far more structured than a Pinterest board [2].

Key asymmetry: Revit gives structured data that needs verification. Pinterest gives inspiration that needs identification. Both feed Phase 1. Neither is ready for procurement. The specifier’s job is the same either way: turn a reference into a row with a real SKU, supplier, and lead time.

Part 5. The intent-to-product funnel

Every product in every spec passes through this sequence — whether the specifier spent twenty minutes on it or two weeks. The funnel widens as it descends, which is counterintuitive. Most people expect narrowing: many ideas converge to one product. What actually happens is the opposite: a thin signal (one image) must generate more and more data to fill the row [3].

The funnel widens at each stage: more data is required, not less. A spec is a database — and this is how each row earns its place in it.

By the time a line item is in the spec, it knows more about the chair than the client’s moodboard ever did. That’s Phase 1 in one sentence. The specifier’s job isn’t to filter down from many options to one — it’s to generate the data that turns a feeling into something the procurement team can actually send to a supplier.

A spec isn’t a document. It’s a database that happens to know how to print itself on paper — and Phase 1 is the moment each row has to earn that right.

Part 6. Why a picture isn’t data — and what that costs

Every engineer has encountered a version of this problem: a client submits a design request as a screenshot with annotations in the margin reading “like this but smaller” and “the blue here should be more of a teal.” A screenshot can’t go through a build pipeline. It has to be translated first.

Phase 1 in FF&E is the same translation problem, at the scale of a thousand objects, over twelve to twenty-four months [4]. The cost of a bad translation isn’t a wrong color in a UI. It’s sixty armchairs arriving at a warehouse in the wrong finish.

What the image has What the spec row needs Who bridges the gap
A chair in a shade that looks like amber Finish code: BRZ-22 Antique Brass Dana calls the sales rep
A lamp “in the style of” mid-century design Manufacturer + model + SKU Dana searches, finds six options, picks one
A rug in a Pinterest screenshot Size + material + source + lead time Dana (or Priya, on a good day)
A hotel lobby shot by a travel blogger Room-by-room area assignments with quantities Dana reconstructs from scale and visual context

Every row in that table is a human decision. It can be supported by software — a web clipper that pulls product data from supplier sites, a catalog search, a match against known products. But the decision itself belongs to the specifier. This is not a gap Fohlio closes by automation; it’s a gap Fohlio supports by giving the specifier the right tools once the decision is made. The distinction matters for understanding what we build and why.

Chapter summary

Phase 1 is the unstructured upstream. The domain starts here, but the database doesn’t yet exist. The specifier’s job is translation: intent to product, image to row, feeling to data.

🖼
Design intent
The answer to “what should this space feel like?” Predates products, prices, and measurements.
📌
Moodboard
Images, not data. Communicates aesthetic direction. Contains no SKUs, lead times, or prices.
🔗
Pinterest / Revit
Consumer inspiration vs. architectural BIM. Both are starting points. Both require specifier translation before entering the spec.
📋
First line item
Product + finish + source + lead time + quantity. Now it’s a row. Now it’s the database.
