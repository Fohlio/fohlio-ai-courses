The Library and the Takeoff

Chapter 5. Why firms that start from scratch every project are paying a tax on their own memory

Helen pulls up the FF&E schedule for the studio’s newest project — a 180-room select-service hotel, brand-new client — and clicks to the guestroom nightstand row. The cell is empty. She opens the shared drive. Eight folders in, she finds a spec sheet from a project two years ago. Same manufacturer, same finish, probably the same item. Maybe. The contact name on the file is someone who left the firm.

She sends the file to Priya with a sticky note: “Use this as a starting point. Verify the lead time with the rep — it may have changed.”

Priya stares at the email. Verify the lead time. With which rep? The spec sheet lists no rep. It lists a manufacturer and a model number and a finish code. The price is from 2022.

This is the moment that costs firms time, money, and accuracy on every single project. Not because the information doesn’t exist — it does, somewhere — but because it lives in spreadsheets, email threads, shared drives, and people’s heads, and half of those people have left the firm. The library is the attempt to solve this problem. The takeoff is the other half of the job: once you know what to specify, you still have to count exactly how many go into the building.

Part 1. The problem with starting from scratch

Helen has been running her studio for fourteen years. In that time, the studio has specified roughly the same set of guestroom nightstand families on twelve projects. Bernhardt, Kimball, one custom millwork shop in North Carolina. Each time, someone has built a spec sheet from scratch — called the rep, confirmed dimensions, updated the price, typed the lead time into a cell. Each project, four or five hours of work for an item the studio knows well.

Twelve projects. Fourteen years. The studio has rebuilt the same nightstand spec from scratch twelve times.

This is not an unusual studio. This is standard practice at most firms. The industry is aware of it, and it is one of the most commonly cited sources of wasted time in the FF&E business [1]:

The root problem: product knowledge lives in scattered spreadsheets, PDFs, email threads, and people’s heads. When someone leaves, a piece of the library leaves with them. When a vendor changes a lead time, nobody knows how many open specs are now wrong. Each new project starts from the same blank page, burning hours on work that was done — and then discarded — on the last project.

The solution the industry has developed — informally for decades, and now in dedicated tools — is a product library: a firm’s private, maintained collection of product knowledge that grows from real project work and can be reused on the next one. The nightstand spec you built on the Tucson hotel becomes the starting point for the Denver hotel. Updated once, it stays accurate for everyone.

Part 2. What a product library actually contains

A product library is not a folder of PDFs. It is a structured collection of records, and the structure matters because unstructured knowledge — the kind in folders and email threads — is the problem the library is meant to solve.

The basic building block is a product line (sometimes called a “family”): a product as a designer thinks of it. Not every possible option — just the core concept. “Bernhardt Stratum Lounge Chair” is a family. Within that family sit specific variants or options: the Stratum in Grade 2 fabric (Slate), the Stratum in Grade 3 leather (Cognac), the Stratum in COM. Each is a distinct purchasable item with its own finish, lead time, and price.

Family = the product concept. Variants = the specific purchasable options. Each variant carries its own pricing, lead times, and material links.

Materials: the components a product is made of

A library record also tracks the materials a product uses. This matters for two reasons.

First, materials change. If a leather grade is discontinued, a designer who has used it on three projects in the past two years needs to know which chairs, in which rooms, on which active projects, are affected — not just the one they are currently specifying. A library that links materials to the products using them makes that question answerable in minutes. Without it, someone has to search every old spec sheet by hand.

Second, COM (Customer’s Own Material) configurations — where the designer specifies the manufacturer’s frame but supplies a separate fabric or leather — require coordination between two supply chains simultaneously. The library holds both the frame spec and the material spec so neither side gets lost in a separate folder [2].

Parts and BOM: what a product is made of

Some FF&E items are not a single object. A custom bed package — standard in hospitality — consists of a frame, a mattress, a mattress topper, and a headboard. Four separate items, each with its own vendor, lead time, and fire-rating certificate. They arrive separately and are assembled on site. The bill of materials (BOM) lists these components and their quantities so the purchasing agent knows to track four POs, not one, and so the receiving team knows what should arrive from four different trucks before that bed can be checked off [3].

Why the BOM matters in practice: if the mattress topper arrives damaged and no one tracks it as a separate line, the receiving team may sign off on “the bed” as received — and find out on install day that the topper is missing. In a hotel with 200 beds, that is 200 separate receiving checks, not 200 “beds.” The BOM is what creates that granularity.

🛏
Helen, reviewing a spec for a custom bed package with Priya: “Where is the mattress topper on this receiving checklist?”
Priya: “It’s part of the bed.”
Helen: “Is it on a separate truck from a separate factory?”
Priya: “Yes.”
Helen: “Then it’s not ‘part of the bed.’ It’s a line item.”
A bed that arrives in four pieces from four vendors requires four lines in the receiving report, or one of them gets lost in the gap — and the whole package fails inspection. The BOM is not bureaucracy. It is the map.

Part 3. The takeoff — counting the building

The library tells you what to specify. The takeoff tells you how many.

A quantity takeoff (in FF&E, often just called the “takeoff”) is the systematic process of counting how many of each item type go in each room type across the whole building, and rolling those counts up to a total order quantity [3]. It is the work that separates “we need lounge chairs” from “we need 180 lounge chairs, in three different finish variants, distributed across six room types, to arrive in two phases.”

In Lesson 3 we followed one chair through all phases of a project. The takeoff is the moment when that one chair becomes a specific number, tied to a specific room, with a specific total that Marco will turn into purchase orders [2].

How the math works: room types roll up

A real hotel does not have 200 identical rooms. It has room types: Standard King, Standard Queen, Studio Suite, Corner Suite, and so on. Each type has its own furniture plan. The takeoff starts at the room-type level and multiplies up to the building total.

Each room type has a furniture plan; multiplying by room count gives the type subtotal; all subtotals sum to the building order quantity.

Packing and overage: the math the spec sheet doesn’t cover

Once you have a raw quantity, two adjustments always happen before the order is placed.

First, packing. Some items are sold by the unit. Others are sold by the box, case, or pallet. A tile specification will list a coverage per box (say, 10 square feet per box). If a project needs 3,400 square feet, the order is not “3,400 tiles” — it is “340 boxes.” And because you cannot order a fraction of a box, you round up. Every purchasing agent deals with this daily [3].

Second, overage (also called wastage or attrition percentage). For any material that gets cut, trimmed, or installed (tile, carpet, wallcovering), a buffer is added — typically 10–25% — to account for cuts at edges, damaged pieces, and pattern matching. On a 3,400-square-foot tile order with 15% overage, the order becomes 3,910 square feet — rounded up to 392 boxes [3].

For upholstered items, the attrition logic is different: it is not wastage in installation, but rather a small buffer of extra units held in reserve for damage on delivery or punch-list replacements. A project ordering 180 lounge chairs might add 3–5 extra to account for pieces damaged in transit that need replacing before the opening date.

✋ Calculate before continuing
A guest bathroom needs 420 square feet of wall tile. The tile comes in boxes of 12 square feet each. The project carries a 20% overage. (a) What is the adjusted square footage after overage? (b) How many boxes must you order? (c) If the purchasing agent orders exactly the raw quantity (35 boxes, no overage), what risk does the hotel take?
(a) 420 × 1.20 = 504 sq ft; (b) 504 ÷ 12 = 42 boxes; (c) if a tile batch is damaged or miscut, there is no buffer — a second order from the same dye lot may be impossible, forcing a substitute finish.

Part 4. The library grows from real work

The elegant part of how product libraries work in practice is that they compound. A library that starts empty gets richer with every project that uses it.

When Priya builds a new nightstand spec — researching the vendor, confirming dimensions, getting the current trade price and lead time — that work does not have to vanish into a project folder when the hotel opens. It can be promoted back into the studio’s library as a verified, ready-to-use record. The next project that needs a similar nightstand starts not from a blank row but from a pre-filled record with a known vendor, known lead time, and a price that just needs a current-year check.

Helen has been thinking about this for years. The studio’s institutional knowledge — the vendors who deliver reliably, the manufacturers whose lead times are accurate, the finish grades that hold up in high-traffic environments — used to leave the door when people left the firm. A structured library is the attempt to make that knowledge durable.

A library that grows from real project work is worth more than any amount of product research done on a blank slate. The question is whether you have a place to put it.

In practice, most firms today still rely on shared drives and email chains — the “scattered spreadsheets, PDFs, and people’s heads” that the industry acknowledges as the incumbent approach [1]. The practical switch point, as the industry has benchmarked it, is around 100 items or three concurrent projects: below that, a shared folder works; above it, the version-control and search problems start costing real time [1]. Tools that manage reusable spec libraries — whether Fohlio, Programa, Studio Designer, or a well-structured Airtable — are all trying to solve the same organizational pain.

Part 5. OS&E — vastly more SKUs, never ends

Everything we have discussed about the takeoff and the library applies to FF&E — the durable furnishings. But there is a second track running alongside it that is larger in item count, faster in cadence, and never truly complete: OS&E.

Recall from Lesson 1: OS&E (Operating Supplies & Equipment) is the consumable, operational layer — towels, linens, glassware, tableware, guest amenities, uniforms, cleaning supplies. It is an operating expense, not a capital asset. And the phrase “vastly more SKUs, never ends” is worth unpacking, because both parts are literally true [4].

Vastly more SKUs: a 200-room hotel’s FF&E specification might run 2,000 line items. Its OS&E opening order might run 8,000–15,000 SKUs. The bathroom alone has a dozen consumable categories: toilet tissue (multiple ply grades), facial tissue, soap bars, shampoo, conditioner, body lotion, shower caps, sewing kits, shoe mitts, dental kits, bath salts, cotton rounds. Each has a primary product, a backup SKU, a par level, and a reorder quantity. Multiply by 200 rooms, two restaurants, a fitness center, and a spa.

Never ends: FF&E refreshes every 7–10 years. OS&E replenishes continuously. The towels wear out. The glassware breaks. Guest amenities are restocked weekly. A hotel’s OS&E program is not a project with a finish date; it is a permanent operations process with its own budget line, its own vendor relationships, and its own buyer. On large projects, the same purchasing agent who procures the FF&E also handles the opening OS&E order — which means the takeoff Helen is managing for furniture is running simultaneously with an even larger inventory exercise for supplies [4].

Why this matters for the GTM team: when a prospect says “we handle OS&E in-house,” they are describing a permanent, ongoing procurement operation that never closes. The sales conversation about OS&E is a different conversation than the one about an FF&E renovation project — and the volume of data involved is an order of magnitude larger.

Part 6. Reusable knowledge in practice

Helen finishes the takeoff for the nightstand. 280 units total across three room types. Two finish variants: walnut veneer for Standard Kings and Queens, lacquered white for Corner Suites. Both variants are from Kimball Hospitality. Lead time confirmed: 12 weeks for the walnut, 14 for the lacquer (the lacquer curing process takes longer).

She saves the updated record to the studio’s library: the family, both variants, the two sources (Kimball direct and the regional dealer), the current trade price, the lead times, and a note that the lacquer variant requires a 14-week buffer. The next designer who opens a hotel project and searches for a nightstand will find this record, verified in 2026, with a contact name that still works at Kimball.

The library is not a product catalog. It is the studio’s accumulated judgment about which products have been specced, what they cost, how long they take, and who to call. It is a form of institutional memory that, if maintained, compounds in value every year the firm is in business.

This is the principle behind dedicated spec library tools — whether a studio uses Fohlio, Programa, Studio Designer, or a carefully maintained Airtable. The tool does not create the knowledge; the projects do. The tool’s job is to make sure the knowledge does not disappear when the project ends [5].

(a) Do the room-type math and arrive at the building totals for desks, lounge chairs, and nightstands. (b) Apply a 5% overage buffer to all items (round up to the nearest whole unit).     ✋ Model answer — open after your own attempt
    (a) Desks: 60+30=90; Lounge chairs: 60×1+30×0=60; Nightstands: 60×2+30×2=180. (b) With 5% overage: Desks: 90×1.05=94.5→95; Lounge chairs: 60×1.05=63; Nightstands: 180×1.05=189. (c) 189 nightstands ÷ 4 per case = 47.25 → 48 cases (always round up; partial cases are not available). (d) If a piece is damaged on delivery or punch-listed as defective, there is no time to place a new order and wait for lead time — the buffer units allow the hotel to open on schedule while the replacement is processed.
