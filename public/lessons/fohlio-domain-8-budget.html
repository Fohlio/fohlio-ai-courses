The Budget That Quietly Grows

Chapter 8. The owner's rep sees a number she doesn't recognize

Sam is the owner's representative on this project — she is the person the investor hired to make sure nothing goes sideways without someone accountable catching it. She has been in this role for eleven years. She can read a budget the way a doctor reads an X-ray: at a glance, something is wrong.

The wrong thing is this: the FF&E budget on slide four shows $1,240,000. The number Marco brought to the kickoff meeting three months ago was $980,000. No scope was added. No decisions changed. Nothing new was specified. And yet the budget grew by $260,000 while everyone was looking at other things.

Sam calls Marco. Marco does not panic. He has seen this before, in every project he has ever worked on. “The number on slide one was always the bare product price,” he says. “This is what it actually costs to get the furniture into the building.”

That gap — between what a product costs on a price sheet and what it costs standing in a room — is what this chapter is about.

Part 1. How a budget starts: the per-key number

Before a single item is specified, before Dana has drawn a moodboard, before anyone has called a vendor, the owner needs a number. The whole industry converges on one primary metric to produce it: FF&E cost per key — cost per guest room [1].

The per-key number gives the owner a sanity check against comparable projects and lets the design team know whether they are working on a $12,000-per-room budget or a $45,000-per-room budget before they fall in love with any particular chair. It is built from historical benchmarks — what similar properties have actually spent — not from a wish list.

The per-key ladder scales steeply. An extra “one tier up” decision can double the FF&E budget of a 100-room hotel.

Once the per-key number is settled, the budget splits into categories: guestroom casegoods and soft goods, public-area seating and lighting, window treatments, artwork and mirrors, technology (TVs, minibars), and a separate bucket for OS&E (the consumables from Lesson 1 — towels, glassware, amenities). Each category gets an allocation. The total is the project budget — and it is this number that Sam watches on every project call [2].

Part 2. Hard costs vs soft costs — and why the soft ones bite hardest

An FF&E budget has two layers. People often act as if only one of them is real.

Hard costs are the items themselves: the furniture, the lighting fixtures, the casegoods, and the labor to install them. These are the rows on Dana’s spec sheet — the things you can point to in a room. They are also the numbers that tend to appear on the early estimate, because they are the easiest to visualize [3].

Soft costs are everything else required to get those items into the building: freight charges, import duties, warehousing, receiving, the design firm’s fee, the purchasing agent’s fee, permit costs, project management, and — critically — the contingency buffer. The word “soft” is misleading. These costs are not optional, they are not estimates, and they do not stay politely in the background. They are frequently the difference between a budget that holds and one that blows [3].

🛋
Hard costs
The items + install labor
Furniture, lighting, casegoods, artwork, window treatments, TVs, minibars; and the labor to physically install them in the building. These are the capital assets that will sit in the depreciation schedule for seven years.
📄
Soft costs
Everything that moves the items
Freight and shipping; import duties and customs brokerage; warehousing and receiving; the design firm’s fee; the purchasing agent’s fee; permits; project management; taxes; contingency. None of these appear on a spec sheet. All of them appear on the final invoice.

On a typical hospitality FF&E project, soft costs account for 20–30% of the total budget — sometimes more, depending on how far the goods travel and how custom the specifications are [3]. The ratio that Sam watches: logistics and installation alone run 6–25% of FF&E product spend, with hospitality and healthcare at the higher end because multi-floor staging and hard opening dates drive complexity up [4].

🚚
Marco’s internal rule: “Never show an owner a bare product price without the soft costs in the same line of sight. If you do, the soft costs will be news at the worst possible moment — which is always right after the PO is signed.”

Sam once received a “final” budget that listed furniture at $840,000. The actual invoice — with freight, warehousing, receiving, and install — came to $1,230,000. The gap was not a mistake. Nobody had ever put the soft costs in the deck.

Part 3. The landed-cost stack: from price sheet to the room

The single most important number in FF&E budgeting is not the price the manufacturer quotes. It is what the industry calls landed cost: everything required to get the product from the factory floor to the guest room. The difference between the two can be 15–35% on a domestic order and significantly more on an international one [3].

Both figures can change by proclamation with little notice. Always re-verify at ustr.gov and hts.usitc.gov before quoting or budgeting any imported goods. [5]

Part 4. The four states of a budget line — why actual drifts above spec

Every line item in an FF&E budget moves through four states before the project closes. Understanding these four states is how Sam reads a budget the way a doctor reads an X-ray — and it is exactly where the $260,000 gap on her slide four came from [3].

📝
Spec (projected)
Dana’s educated estimate at specification stage — often based on prior project experience or catalog reference prices. Not a real quote.
📨
Quoted
The price a vendor actually provided in response to an RFQ. May be higher or lower than the spec number. Now it is a real market data point.
✅
Approved
The price the owner and designer signed off on. The formal budget commitment. This is the number Sam watches.
💳
Actual
What was actually paid on the purchase order. Often differs from approved because quotes expire, scope creeps in, and landed-cost surprises materialize.

The gap between approved and actual comes from two structural sources. First, quotes expire: a vendor quote is valid for 30–90 days. If the PO takes longer to issue — owner approvals slow, construction schedule shifts — the quote lapses and the new price is higher. Nobody changed the approved number in the budget. It simply became stale. Second, scope creep: the owner adds a console table here, upgrades a fabric there, approves a last-minute accessory package. Each individual ask seems small. Across a project, untracked scope additions typically add 10–15% above the approved budget [3].

Sam pulls up the budget comparison on her laptop. The spec line for the lobby lounge chairs says $48,000. The quoted line says $56,500. The approved line says $56,500 — she had approved it when the quote came in. The actual line says $61,200.

The extra $4,700 is not a mystery. Two chairs were added to the lobby layout after the PO was issued — a last-minute request from the interior designer to fill a corner. Those two chairs triggered a revised order at a slightly higher unit price because the original MOQ had already been hit at the lower quantity. The approval for those two extra chairs happened in an email thread that nobody logged against the budget line.

Sam makes a note: scope approval process broken, not the budget tool.

Part 5. Contingency, value engineering, and the moments they work

Two tools exist specifically to manage the drift between approved and actual. Both are frequently misused by being applied too late.

Contingency: plan for what you can’t predict

A contingency buffer is not padding and it is not a sign of poor planning. It is a deliberate financial reserve for the risks that are real but not yet quantifiable: freight market swings, a finish that arrives damaged and must be reordered, a vendor who misses a production milestone and forces an air-freight upgrade [3]. Standard contingency runs 5–10% of total FF&E budget for residential projects and 10–15% for commercial and hospitality [3]. Sam insists on 12% on every project Marco works. She has never had a project come in under contingency. She has had several that needed every cent of it.

Value engineering: the timing matters as much as the decision

Value engineering — VE — means finding cost-equivalent or lower-cost alternatives that deliver the same functional result: a different fabric supplier, a simpler finish detail, a standard-stock item substituted for a custom one. It is a legitimate and necessary part of the process. It is also almost entirely worthless if it happens after the purchase orders are issued [3].

Late VE requires the purchasing team to go back to vendors, renegotiate or cancel existing orders (sometimes paying restocking fees), restart the sample-approval process, and push out the delivery schedule. That is not cost savings — it is cost generation with extra steps. VE done during the design phase, before Dana finalizes the spec, can shave 10–20% off the hard-cost number without touching the schedule [3].

Part 6. Deposits, payment terms, and the procurement firm as a bank

In Lesson 3 we traced one chair’s journey through the phases — and the financial mechanics behind that journey are straightforward in principle and surprisingly painful in practice.

The standard B2B manufacturing payment pattern is 50% deposit on purchase order acceptance, balance due before shipment — sometimes written “CBS” (collect before shipment) [3]. The deposit starts the production clock. The balance clears customs. The furniture moves. This means the procurement firm is holding substantial cash for weeks or months before the goods arrive and before the owner reimburses them. On a $1M FF&E package, that is $500,000 out the door before a single chair leaves the factory.

Payment terms between firms add another layer. Standard trade terms are Net 30 or Net 60 — the invoice is due 30 or 60 days after receipt. A common variant is 2/10 Net 30: take a 2% discount if you pay within 10 days, otherwise pay the full amount in 30 days. That 2% discount sounds modest until you do the math: annualized, it represents an effective rate of approximately 36.7% — a compelling reason to pay early if you have the liquidity [3].

The practical implication for the procurement firm: it frequently “plays bank” — it pays the vendor on Net 30 terms from its own cash while the owner’s reimbursement may not arrive for weeks after that. This cash-flow exposure is one of the structural reasons why large, well-capitalized purchasing firms like Benjamin West or Stroud Group have a competitive advantage over smaller operations: they can carry the float [2].

Part 7. Tariffs and freight — the budget risks that arrive without warning

In Lesson 7 we saw the three prices for the same chair depending on who is buying. In this chapter, the question is about where the chair is made — because country of origin now carries a budget risk that did not exist in this form five years ago.

Two live risks sit on every imported FF&E line item. Neither is under the control of the designer, the purchasing firm, or the owner:

Tariffs: a cost that can change between specification and PO

US furniture import tariffs in 2025–2026 reached levels the industry had not seen in decades. As of mid-2026 the stacked effective rate on China-origin upholstered furniture — combining the MFN base duty, the Section 301 duty (still in force since 2018 at 25% on List 3), and any Section 232 application — runs roughly 30–35%+ on a single SKU [5]. Vietnam, the main China-alternative for furniture manufacturing, carries a baseline reciprocal tariff of approximately 20%, with transshipments potentially reaching 40% [5].

The practical impact: a project specified in Q1 with a China-sourced furniture package may face a materially different duty bill by the time the purchase order is issued in Q3, if the tariff situation shifts in the interval. A single misclassification or country-of-origin error can swing landed cost by 15–40% [5]. This is why experienced procurement teams now build a tariff-volatility buffer into the contingency, diversify sourcing countries even at modest unit-cost premiums, and re-verify rates immediately before issuing any PO for imported goods.

Numbers are dated and volatile. The tariff landscape described here reflects public information as of mid-2026. Rates change by presidential proclamation with minimal notice. Before budgeting any import line: check the current Harmonized Tariff Schedule at hts.usitc.gov and current USTR trade actions at ustr.gov. Do not rely on any figure in this lesson for a live pricing decision.

Freight: the rate that can move 60% in a quarter

Trans-Pacific ocean freight rates are genuinely volatile — not in the “prices go up a bit” sense but in the sense that West Coast container rates have moved by 60% or more within a single year depending on lane capacity, geopolitical events (the 2024 Red Sea disruption added weeks of transit via the Cape of Good Hope), and fleet dynamics [6]. Budgeting domestic freight at 5–10% of product cost and European or Asian imports at 15–25% is a reasonable starting range for early estimates — but any quote more than 60 days old should be treated as stale [3].

The counterintuitive reality: lower freight rates do not automatically save money on tariff-heavy imports. On a container of China-sourced furniture carrying a 30%+ duty load, a $1,500 improvement in freight cost is arithmetically negligible against a duty bill that runs tens of thousands of dollars on the same container. Certainty of delivery — a domestic manufacturer whose lead time is predictable and whose goods face no import duty — often beats the marginal savings of a cheaper overseas source, especially under a tight opening-date schedule [5].

🚢
Sam, on a project call, after the freight broker updated the quote for the third time in six weeks: “Why does every furniture budget I see have a freight line that says ‘TBD’ until two months before delivery?”
Marco: “Because anyone who gives you a firm freight number six months out is either lying or doesn’t understand ocean freight.”
Sam added a 15% freight-volatility line to the contingency that afternoon. It was the first time in five years she had not needed it. The year after that, she needed every cent of it.

Chapter summary

Sam’s $260,000 gap was not a mistake. It was what happens when a budget starts with bare product prices and the landed-cost stack, soft costs, scope creep, and tariff exposure are treated as someone else’s problem. This chapter named every layer of that problem.
